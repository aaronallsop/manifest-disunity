# Diplomacy — ideation (round 5)

**Stage 1 of five: IDEATION. Nothing here is judged, chosen, sized or ruled on.**

**Status: OPEN, 14 September 2026.** Round 4 closed on the Control Board at 21:06 the same evening.
This is the idea bank for how nations deal with one another: recognition, alliances, blocs and
federations, vassalage, petitions, thaws, and what a brand-new country does when nobody will talk to
it.

**The rule for this document:** an idea earns a place by having been had. It does not have to be
good, affordable, consistent with its neighbours, or compatible with what is already built. Several
entries below flatly contradict each other and that is correct at this stage.

**Ideas are numbered T1, T2, … — T for *talks*.** S, C, P and E are taken by rounds 1–4, and D is
already the prefix of every entry in `DECISIONS.md`. Using D here would produce two different things
both called D166.

## 0a. The three things written at the top of every round

**One action per nation per turn, and it ends the turn.** Six components compete for one slot. Any
idea below that assumes a decision every turn has taken that turn away from the other five, and this
round has to say so out loud. **Round 4 already overspent this budget badly** — the economy alone
wants ten verbs — and **six of the game's eleven existing player moves are already diplomatic**
(§3a). This round is the one most at risk of pretending the budget does not exist.

**The board and the ideologies are fixed.** Geography is baked from federal data. If this round finds
itself wanting to change either, that is a finding worth stopping for.

**Every quantity says where its number comes from** — measured from a named file, invented as a
placeholder, or still to be asked about.

## 3. What is actually built today — verified 14 September 2026

**Read before proposing anything.** Every line here was checked against the code this session, not
remembered. A design round writes documents only; this is reading, not touching.

### 3a. Six of the game's eleven player moves are already diplomatic

The move registry holds eleven planners: **annex, unite, release, govern, autonomy, recognise,
trade, treaty, aid, transit, revoke.** Six of those — recognise, trade, treaty, aid, transit,
revoke — are diplomacy. **This round's budget problem is not that it has no verbs. It is that it
already has more than any other system, against a budget of one action a turn.**

### 3b. Relations is one append-only list, and there is no stance machine

Every feeling between two nations is a decaying, **directed** memory in a single list, scored as
`base + Σ magnitude × decay^age`, clamped to −1…+1. The bands are **labels printed on that score** —
Close, Warm, Indifferent, Cold, **Hostile** — and nothing else.

⚠ **There is no Hostile / Cease-fire / Wary / Cordial / Allied state machine in the game.** Conquest's
ruling 7 table is a **design**, not a build. So is "thirty-three pairs open Hostile": that number is
asserted from ruling 19's claimant fields — five Texans, five Californians, five Confederate
claimants and three capital contenders, ten plus ten plus ten plus three — and **was never measured
from data.** *This matters for item (a): a thaw action may be as cheap as writing one of five
existing good memories, or as expensive as inventing the state machine first.*

### 3c. Only ONE nation opens unrecognised, and it is not in Texas

Recognition returns **true unconditionally for every nation the game opened with.** The matrix only
holds nations founded during play, and the shattered scenario founds exactly one: **Deseret**, which
opens as a pariah with a parent, and whose own note says *"Utah does NOT open refusing recognition:
the relations board starts quiet and the player watches it sour."*

⚠ **So conquest ruling 19 has not been built.** Austin's veto, the four rebels' pariah status, and
the four-way auction for Austin's signature (C97, C98) — *"the most interesting corner of the map"* —
**do not exist in the game today.** Conquest recorded this as a departure needing a change to
scenario **content**. Nothing has changed it. It is item (j), and it is a content job, not a code job.

### 3d. What being a pariah costs, as built

Four things, all live: no bilateral trade with anyone who does not recognise you; **a smuggler's rate
on the world market** rather than a lock — the cut shrinks to nothing as the world comes round; no
seat in a coalition; and a standing deficit on Influence. Recognition is **earned every turn** — by
standing, kinship, having lasted, being too big to ignore, and **worth more than all of them
combined, the parent giving in.**

### 3e. ⚠ Patronage already exists, and vassalage is not a blank page

**This is the find of the session, and neither the conquest round nor this document's stub knew it.**
The aid move does not merely transfer money. It creates a **patron**: the donor's ideology is blended
into the recipient's own government lean, weighted by how recently and how much they were paid,
decaying every turn they are not.

- **One patron at a time**, and a newcomer must **outbid** the incumbent. *"A client state is
  somebody's client."*
- **The same money buys far more of a small country than a large one** — the weight is the donation
  as a share of the recipient's annual income.
- **A patron who stops paying stops being one.**
- The code can already answer "who is in my sphere."

**So items 2, (e) and (m) — vassals, Subject, and what submission is — inherit a working
patron/client relation with a bidding war in it.** What is missing is everything *formal*: a vassal
owes nothing, the overlord gets no say, nobody can demand it, and it has no name in the interface.

### 3f. Treaties, and the one that is already a diplomacy puzzle

Two kinds exist. **`nonaggression`** — neither may annex from the other; breaking it is what the
ledger records. **`compact`** — a standing trade understanding where both sides' deals skip the
cooldown, and it is **broken by refusing to recognise.** Treaty standing is *pacts held minus
breaches*, and **a breach is deliberately worth more than a pact**, so a serial betrayer cannot
out-sign their reputation.

### 3g. Coalitions, and what they are not

Coalitions **form only against a threat and dissolve when it passes.** They are a per-turn survey
over the relations list, not an agreement anybody signs. **Every deal in the game is bilateral.**
There is no standing multilateral object of any kind — **which is exactly the hole the Farmers Union,
the federation and every bloc idea has to fill.**

## 5. The idea bank

*Numbered T1, T2, … Unjudged. The questions in §4 are not ideas and are not numbered here.*

**Sources**: the story of the break-up (`secession-ideation.md` §8), the in-tray in §2, what the code
actually does (§3), `FUTURE-IDEAS.md` F16, F18, F22 and F23, and the rounds themselves. Where an idea
contradicts its neighbour, both stay.

### A. The thaw, and the vocabulary of getting on

**T1 — The thaw is an action that writes an existing memory, and nothing else is needed.** C99's
find: the memory list already carries five *good* kinds — `granted`, `traded`, `recognised`,
`treatied`, `aided` — each with a tuned weight. A diplomatic overture writes one of them. No new
ledger, no new state, no new arithmetic. *The cheapest version of item (a) by a wide margin.*

**T2 — The thaw is a sixth memory kind of its own: `courted`.** Distinct from the five, because those
five are all *side effects of doing something else* — you traded, so they remember trade. An overture
is the only entry whose entire content is "we tried". It should be weaker than any of the five and
**repeatable**, which none of the five are.

**T3 — The thaw does not change the score; it changes the *decay rate*.** Aaron's own words are
*"speed things up"*, not *"improve relations"*. A grudge that would have taken forty turns to fade
takes twenty. **This is the reading closest to what he said** and it is a different mechanism from
T1 and T2 — it multiplies time rather than adding a number.

**T4 — The thaw is refusable, and a refused overture is worse than none.** You spend the turn, they
say no, and the world writes `reneged`-shaped memory against *you* for the presumption. *Makes the
act a risk rather than a free tick, which is what stops it being spammed.*

**T5 — Only the weaker party may make the first overture.** Diplomacy from the stronger nation reads
as a demand. Mechanically: the overture is available only to the nation with less weight, which means
**Austin, bankrupt and encircled, is the one nation that can always talk.**

**T6 — A third nation can mediate, and takes the credit.** A pair cannot reach each other, but a
nation on good terms with both can spend its turn improving *their* relation and writes a `granted`
against both of them. *This is the only idea in the bank where one action moves three nations, which
is either the answer to the action budget or a reason to reject it.*

**T7 — Hostility has a floor that the thaw cannot cross, and that floor is the contest.** Conquest
already ruled it: the reunification contests are *"a floor, not a rate"*, and Hostile is the best
those pairs can ever be until the contest ends. **So the thaw does nothing for thirty-three of the
pairs it was invented for** — which is a problem this round has to face, not route around.

**T8 — The state machine is built first, or it is never built.** Against T1: conquest designed seven
relationship states and the game has none of them. Doing the thaw as a memory entry is cheap
*because* it quietly abandons the state machine. **Somebody should say out loud whether the seven
states are still the design.** *(See F18 — Aaron has already thought about making the attitudes
one-way, which only means anything once there are attitudes.)*

### B. Recognition as a currency

**T9 — Recognition is the only unilateral act in the game, and that is its whole value.** No
agreement, no negotiation, no state requirement, no money, no action cost. **It is the only thing two
hostile nations can do to each other that is not violence.** Everything else in this round is an
offer somebody can refuse.

**T10 — Recognition should cost the turn's action after all.** Against T9 and against the build. Free
recognition means the AI can sign everybody on turn one and the asset evaporates. *Round 3 set the
precedent — ruling 19 made proposing a union cost the turn "by precedent rather than by a new
decision".*

**T11 — Recognition can be *withdrawn*.** Nothing in the build allows it. De-recognition would be the
sharpest non-violent weapon available: it cuts bilateral trade, the coalition seat and the market
rate in one move, and it writes a memory worse than never having signed — **exactly the asymmetry
already built into treaty breaches.**

**T12 — Recognition can be *sold*, and Austin's game is the auction.** C97 and C98: four nations each
need their own copy of the same signature from a nation that hates all four. The price is corridors,
money, or an end to a claim. *Cannot be tested today — §3c — because ruling 19 is not built.*

**T13 — Recognition can be *conditional*: I sign when you drop your claim.** Which makes it the first
instrument in the game where the payment is *an act rather than a thing*.

**T14 — Recognising somebody is an insult to their parent, and that is already built.** `betrayed`
fires against the parent whenever a third party signs. **So recognition is simultaneously a gift and
an attack, and the same action does both.** *This is the richest thing in the built system and no
round has used it.*

**T15 — What you get for recognising is a claim on their gratitude, and gratitude is spendable.** The
`recognised` memory is directed — *they* owe *you*. Item (c) answered as: recognition is how a weak
nation buys a favour it can call in later.

**T16 — Collective recognition: a bloc recognises as one.** If a multilateral object exists (§E),
then joining it delivers every member's signature at once — which makes membership the fastest route
out of pariah status and gives blocs a reason to exist that is not economic.

### C. The offer — petitions, ultimatums, proposals, demands

**T17 — There is ONE offer object, and five things wear it.** A petition, an ultimatum, a proposal of
union, a demand of submission and an offer of alliance are all: *a thing offered, held open for a
term, refusable, withdrawable, and possibly bid for by a rival.* **The build already has this shape**
— negotiation with a term, a fixed price and an expiry. *The most important structural idea in the
round, because it decides whether this is one mechanism or five.*

**T18 — An offer has three answers, not two: yes, no, and not yet.** Politics ruling 16 already ruled
this for movements and called the middle one the interesting one. **The same three answers should
serve nation-to-nation offers**, and *not yet* is what makes an offer a standing thing rather than a
coin flip.

**T19 — The petition threshold is a separate, lower number than the secession threshold, and the gap
is the whole game.** Finding A. If they are equal, nothing ever asks. If the gap is wide, every
movement spends years asking before it ever declares — which is **the entire middle act of the
game**, and today it does not exist.

**T20 — The gap is not a constant; it is set by how the government has behaved.** A government that
has answered petitions before gets asked again. One that has crushed them is declared against without
warning. *Makes the threshold a consequence rather than a tunable.*

**T21 — Several nations can be petitioned at once and they compete.** Secession ruling 50, already
ruled. **What this round owes is the auction**: do they bid, does the movement pick, is it decided by
nerve or by arithmetic? Aaron said nerve.

**T22 — Accepting a petition costs the accepting nation what it was.** Already ruled: El Paso gives
up its claim to reunify Texas; Oregon stops being Oregon. *So the bid is paid in identity, not money
— the only price of that kind in the game.*

**T23 — Refusing costs nothing now and everything later.** Already ruled. **The mechanism owed here
is the memory**: which entry does a refused movement write, and against whom? `betrayed` exists for a
related injury. *And does a refused movement remember, or does its nation?*

**T24 — A nation can solicit a petition rather than wait to be asked.** S99, the other half of
sponsorship. You spend to make a movement inside your neighbour, then you invite it. **Sponsorship
and petition are the two ends of one act and nobody has connected them.**

**T25 — An ultimatum is an offer with a threat attached, and the threat is what prices it.** Houston
to Louisiana: submit, or be invaded. *Mechanically it is T17 plus a declared consequence the offerer
is then committed to — and being committed is the point. An ultimatum you walk away from should cost
you standing with everyone watching.*

**T26 — Everyone can see an offer, or nobody can.** Secession ruling 21 says sponsorship is visible
to all. Is an ultimatum? A petition? **The board's whole atmosphere depends on this and it has never
been asked.** *(Related: item (q), what a player can see of another nation — filed to the design
stage.)*

### D. Alliances

**T27 — An alliance is made the way a trade deal is made, and that is enough.** Negotiation, a term,
an expiry, a renegotiation prompt. Conquest ruling 27 already said what it *does*; the making of it
needs no new machinery.

**T28 — An alliance has no term, and ending one is the interesting act.** Against T27. A pact that
merely expires is weather. A pact somebody has to *break* writes `reneged`, and the build already
punishes that harder than never signing.

**T29 — You cannot ally with someone you are hostile to, so alliances are only ever made between
nations that did not need one.** A structural criticism of ruling 27, not an idea — **but it means
the thaw (§A) is the gateway to every alliance on the board**, which raises the stakes on item (a)
considerably.

**T30 — An inherited quarrel decays like any other, and that is why one hop is enough.** C132,
unanswered since 9 September. *If an inherited grudge does NOT decay, a long game accumulates
permanent hostility through friends-of-friends and the board freezes.*

**T31 — Against T30: an inherited quarrel does not decay while the alliance stands, and evaporates
the moment it ends.** Truer to what it is — you dislike them *because* of your friend — and it makes
leaving an alliance a way to clean your slate, which is a real move.

**T32 — Allies see each other's board.** Deliberately absent from ruling 27. *Filed here because it
is the obvious thing to ask for next and somebody will.*

**T33 — An alliance can be one-sided: a guarantee.** I promise to defend you; you promise nothing.
Conquest items (d) and F16 both point at this. **It is the shape of the Canadian-protection idea and
of every protectorate**, and it is the bridge between §D and §F.

### E. Blocs and federations

**T34 — There is exactly one multilateral form, and it is the federation.** Politics ruled it in
full. Everything looser — the Farmers Union, a customs union, a defensive pact of three — is either a
federation or a pile of bilateral deals. *Cheapest answer to question 4, and it makes ruling 34's
floor of three the floor for everything.*

**T35 — Against T34: there are two forms, and the second is the one the story actually describes.**
The Farmers Union is **seven governors signing a thing, not a state**. It has no leader, no turn, no
toll, no election. **A federation is heavy; a bloc is light**, and the story has three light ones and
one heavy one.

**T36 — A bloc is a standing multilateral *deal*, not a government.** Free transit inside, a common
front outside, and nothing else. No leader, no budget, no turn. *Answers E87 by subtraction.*

**T37 — Joining a bloc is the concede-less answer to a Unify movement.** Politics found this while
answering its own inbox: integration without a merger. **So a bloc is not only an economic object —
it is one of the prices a government can pay to keep its ground.**

**T38 — Leaving a bloc is what the story is actually about.** Chicago left the Farmers Union and took
north-west Indiana with it. **The interesting rules are the exit rules**, and ruling 34 already says
anyone may walk out. *What it costs to walk out is open.*

**T39 — A bloc can expel.** Nothing in ruling 34 allows it — you may leave, but nobody may push you.
**An expulsion is the multilateral version of de-recognition (T11)** and it would give a bloc teeth
it currently lacks.

**T40 — A bloc admits by vote, and the vote is the drama.** Ruling 34: the members vote you in. *The
owed mechanism is what they vote on — size? politics? whether they like you? — and whether one
member can veto.*

**T41 — The federation's flat toll and the built corridor system are different animals, and one has
to give.** Round 3 flagged it and did not solve it: corridors are negotiated, per-mode,
per-direction, with a notice period, and tolls **compound**. A flat, automatic, unrefusable 10% is
something else. **Either the federation overrides the corridor system inside itself, or the corridor
system has to learn a second mode.**

**T42 — A federation can string a member along.** F22, found during round 3. The federation may
refuse a member's petition for war — and *refusing indefinitely* is the same shape as a government
stringing a movement along, whose escalation is **leaving**. *A federation destroyed from the inside
by its own caution, with no rule about federations breaking up.*

**T43 — Members quietly resent each other.** F23. Rulings 27 and 28 guarantee it — the money flows to
the ground that is crossed, and the members elect a leader who spends their budget. Ruling 30 says
members are simply at peace. **A federation of people who resent each other is the truer picture**,
and it costs nothing to say so.

**T44 — The Farmers Union's corridors ARE its purpose, and they are on the map.** The story: the
governors signed to protect *"their corridors to Canada and down the Mississippi."* **Round 3
measured that a united farm state would hold three of the game's fifteen chokepoints, Cairo among
them.** So a bloc is a thing that owns chokepoints collectively — and that is a different object from
a nation that owns them.

### F. Vassals, patrons and spheres

**T45 — Vassalage already exists and is called aid.** §3e. A patron's ideology bleeds into the
client's government; one patron at a time; a newcomer must outbid the incumbent; a patron who stops
paying stops being one. **The question is not how to build a client state. It is whether to give the
built one a name and a formal layer.**

**T46 — The formal layer is: the vassal loses its foreign policy and keeps everything else.**
Politics ruling 39 already ruled the domestic half — it keeps its own government, and its people
blame *that* government. *So the overlord takes the foreign column and nothing else.*

**T47 — What the overlord gets is the buffer, and the buffer is spatial.** Oklahoma was worth having
because it sat between Dallas and everyone north. **Mechanically: an attack on the vassal is an
attack the overlord sees coming a turn early**, which is a real benefit that costs the game nothing
to express.

**T48 — What the overlord gets is the vassal's *action*.** C112's problem, stated as a feature: the
overlord spends the vassal's turn. **For an AI vassal this is enormous and for a player vassal it
ends the game**, which is exactly why conquest deferred it. *Two answers are possible — vassalage is
AI-only, or a vassal keeps its action and owes something else.*

**T49 — A vassal keeps its action and owes a tithe.** The gentler answer to T48: a share of income,
transit at no toll, and a veto the overlord can spend on the vassal's offers.

**T50 — Vassalage can be demanded, and refusing it is a casus belli.** The story, exactly: Houston
demanded it of Louisiana, was refused, and invaded. **This is T25, the ultimatum, pointed at
sovereignty** — and it is how the story's largest war started.

**T51 — Vassalage can be *offered upward*.** A small nation that cannot survive alone asks a big one
to take it. *The reverse of T50 and the more interesting one, because the player is the one who has
to decide what they are prepared to stop being.* **This is the petition mechanism (§C) with a nation
in place of a movement.**

**T52 — A patron can be outbid, and the bidding war is visible.** Already built and nobody has looked
at it. **Two nations pouring money into the same third country, each trying to out-pay the other, is
a proxy war with no soldiers in it** — and the game already computes who is winning.

**T53 — A sphere is just the set of your clients, and it should be drawable.** `clientsOf` already
answers it. A map mode that paints spheres would make the whole system legible for the cost of a
colour.

**T54 — Canada or Mexico as an overlord.** F16, deferred by Aaron himself. A border state puts itself
under foreign protection and becomes ground that **cannot be attacked** — the first such ground on
the board. *Requires D168 ruling 2 to be revisited deliberately: Canada and Mexico are geography, not
actors. Recorded, not proposed.*

### G. Money as leverage

**T55 — A creditor may demand the debtor's vote.** Item (r): the economy created leverage and nobody
said what it buys. The cheapest answer — inside a federation or a bloc, a debtor votes with its
creditor.

**T56 — A creditor may demand recognition.** The purest answer, because recognition costs the debtor
nothing material and is worth everything to the creditor. *And it connects §G to §B without new
machinery.*

**T57 — A creditor may demand transit.** The route rather than the money. *Which is what Houston
wanted from Louisiana and what all trade funnelling through Arizona is about.*

**T58 — Debt can be forgiven, and forgiveness is the cheapest friendship in the game.** Writes
`aided`, costs a treasury number, buys standing. **The move a rich nation makes when it wants a
client and does not want a war.**

**T59 — Sponsorship is aid pointed at somebody's rebels, and everybody can see it.** Secession ruling
21, already ruled. **The owed mechanism is what it writes**: `betrayed` is the nearest existing entry
and the round agreed paying rebels is *worse* than recognising them. **So sponsorship needs an entry
heavier than the heaviest one that exists.**

**T60 — Sponsorship draws a coalition.** Coalitions form on threat; funding somebody's rebels should
count as threat. *Free, because the survey already reads the memory list.*

**T61 — You can sponsor a movement and then petition it — and that is the complete aggressive
diplomatic play.** T24 and T59 joined: spend three turns growing a movement inside your neighbour,
then invite it to join you. **No army, no declaration, and the ground changes hands.** *If this works
it is the best thing in the round; if it is too cheap it breaks the map.*

**T62 — Against T61: a sponsored movement remembers who paid it, and it is nobody's friend.**
Secession's own warning, generalised — a movement refused often enough *"stops asking and falls back
to its own verb, becoming a country on your border that remembers."* A bought movement should be able
to turn on its buyer.

### H. Stateless ground and who signs for it

**T63 — Stateless ground has a signatory and it is whoever holds the toll booth.** Aaron: tier-3
zones *"make their own deals."* Wyoming charges tolls on food bound for Deseret and Idaho. **So
somebody signs, and the simplest answer is that the ground itself is the counterparty.**

**T64 — Stateless ground cannot be recognised, because there is nobody to recognise.** Which means it
can trade with anyone and ally with no one — **a permanent neutral through which everything flows.**
*That is a genuinely distinct object and it is six regions of the opening board.*

**T65 — Against T64: a stateless region can be recognised, and recognising it is what stops a
neighbour eating it.** Recognition as a protective act rather than a commercial one.

**T66 — Stateless ground can grant a corridor and take a toll, and the money goes nowhere.** E88's
question answered by subtraction: the toll is a **cost to the shipper and not income to anyone** —
exactly as the Canada corridor's 10% already works. *One built precedent, reused.*

**T67 — Against T66: the toll accumulates, and when it is large enough the ground becomes a
state.** A stateless region that gets rich enough to govern itself. **The only route from tier 3
upward that anyone has proposed.**

**T68 — Nothing is built and this is the largest new mechanic in the story.** Politics said so in its
own inbox: *"Every Area belongs to a nation; there is no nobody's-land."* **Six regions of the story
need it and the game cannot express one of them.** *Recorded as scale, not as an idea.*

### I. The board at turn zero

**T69 — The scenario should author agreements, not only grudges.** Item 6. Today it writes nations,
sentiment and back-dated memories, and its own note says *"the board starts quiet."* **The story has
at least four agreements in place before the game opens** — the New England–Rochester alliance and
its twenty-year free-trade deal, the Farmers Union, Washington–Oregon, and Oklahoma's submission to
Dallas.

**T70 — Conquest ruling 19 is the same argument and it has already been won.** C46 and C65: *"a war
that ended two years ago does not leave two nations at Peace."* **Ruling 19 applied that to
recognition and the change was never made** (§3c, finding A). *Item (j) and item 6 are one job.*

**T71 — A twenty-year free-trade deal is eighty turns, and no deal in the game runs that long.**
Durations are 2 / 4 / 8 / 20. **Either the pre-signed deal is a different object from a trade deal,
or the duration table needs a fifth entry.** *A small concrete collision, worth catching now.*

**T72 — Oklahoma's vassalage is the test case for §F.** It is in the story, it is on the opening
board, and today it is simply a separate nation with no relationship to Dallas at all.

**T73 — The thirty-three hostile pairs should be measured, not asserted.** Finding C. The number
comes from claimant lists — ten Texan pairs, ten Californian, ten Confederate, three for the capital
— and **nobody has checked it against the board the scenario actually builds.** *A data job, half an
hour, and every argument about the thaw rests on it.*

**T74 — Every pre-signed agreement is a thing the player can break on turn one.** Which is the
cheapest way to make the opening board feel like a *situation* rather than a setup.

### J. Reputation, information and what a nation may know

**T75 — Reputation already exists and is called treaty standing.** Pacts held minus breaches, with a
breach weighted heavier so a serial betrayer cannot out-sign their reputation. **The round should
reuse this rather than invent a second number.**

**T76 — A broken trade deal should hurt the same way a broken treaty does.** Aaron, 5 September:
cancelling a deal early *"should damage your reputation with other nations, make other nations
hesitant to create trade deals with you (raising the amount they would want from you and that would
cool down over time)."* **Recorded as F2 and deliberately not built** — a deal can end today only by
expiry, renewal or a nation dying, and there is no way to walk out of one. *Checked this session, not
remembered.*

**T76a — And F2 asks for a reputation number that already exists.** F2 specifies *"a new per-nation
standing that decays toward neutral."* **Treaty standing is exactly that** (§3f) — pacts held minus
breaches, with the breach weighted heavier. **Two reputation numbers would be two different ideas
about what breaking a promise costs**, which is the thing F2's own last paragraph warns against.
*This round should say there is one.*

**T77 — Attitudes run one way.** F18, Aaron's own, deferred. A nation can be hostile toward a
neighbour that has not noticed — *"arguably the position most of the board is in."* **It costs a map
view that shows two colours on a border.**

**T78 — You cannot negotiate with what you cannot see.** If information is limited (round 7), then
every offer in §C is made partly blind, and **scouting becomes a diplomatic act.** *Filed, because
round 7 owns it and this round should not pre-empt it.*

**T79 — The AI should explain its refusals.** Every other number in this game is a *why* record. A
nation that says no should say why, in the same sentence form. *Cheap, and it is the difference
between diplomacy and a slot machine.*

**T80 — There is no diplomacy screen.** Six diplomatic moves exist and they are reached through the
map. **Whatever this round designs has to be reachable**, and that is the design stage's problem the
moment this round closes.

### K. Coercion short of war

**T81 — An embargo is refusing to sell, and it is already a hostility cause.** Conquest listed *a
nation refusing to sell* among its five causes. **So the act exists in the design and not in the
game.**

**T82 — Closing a corridor is the sharpest non-violent weapon on the board, and it is built.** The
revoke move, with a notice period. *Round 4 measured what a lost route costs; this round should
notice that revoke is already the answer to "what do I do about them" for any nation whose enemy
ships through it.*

**T83 — A blockade is conquest's, and finance is the one sector it cannot touch.** F34. Recorded so
the boundary is clear.

**T84 — Collective coercion: a bloc embargoes as one.** The multilateral version of T81, and the
first thing a bloc could do that a pile of bilateral deals cannot.

**T85 — Buying the second supplier is coercion pointed at a third party.** Round 4's measured 12%:
opening one more seller drops the price by about an eighth. **So persuading somebody's supplier to
sell to you as well is an attack on their economy that looks like ordinary trade.** *The most elegant
thing the economy handed this round.*

## 5a. Rulings

### Ruling 1 — The contest floor is per contest, not a rule: Texas and California never thaw, the Confederacy does

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"Texas and Califonia stays hostile the whole game. Confederacy thaws."*

**The question put** was whether a thaw can ever cross the permanent floor the reunification contests
put under their claimants. The answer is not one rule for all four contests. **It is a property of
each contest.**

| Contest | Claimants | Pairs | Ruled |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A Free Texas** | Austin, Dallas, Houston, San Antonio, El Paso | 10 | **Permanent. No thaw, the whole game** |
| **California Republic** | Los Angeles, the Bay Area, Riverside, SoCal, Northern California | 10 | **Permanent. No thaw, the whole game** |
| **The Confederacy** | the Deep South, the Carolinas, the Gulf nation, Appalachia, Central Florida | 10 | **Thaws** |
| **The Thirteen Colonies** | three of Washington D.C., the United States of New England, Philadelphia, New York City — cut to three by round 1's ruling 20 | 3 | **Permanent** — ruling 3 |

**Why this is better than either global answer, and the reason is in the story rather than in the
mechanics.** Texas and California **just fell apart**. Each of those ten pairs is a set of people who
were one country two years ago and are now five, each insisting the other four are illegitimate. That
is not a grudge; it is an unfinished argument, and it does not cool while both sides are still making
it.

**The Confederacy was never one country in living memory.** Its claimants are five distinct regional
nations — the Deep South, the Carolinas, the Gulf, Appalachia, Central Florida — with their own
histories, who would have to *agree* to become something. **And the prize is unreachable anyway:**
round 1 ruled the Confederacy *"cannot be done without Texas, which is five nations away."* A contest
nobody can win is a contest whose claimants have far less reason to hate each other than the ones
tearing at a prize that is right there.

**What this costs and what it buys.** Twenty pairs — a fifth of the board's worst quarrels — keep no
diplomatic exit at all and can be settled only by force, which is the price. What it buys is that
**the two corners of the map built around a succession war stay wars**, and the south stays a place
where alliances can form. *Finding E is therefore half closed: the thaw is barred where it was meant
to be barred, and free where the game needs it.*

*Ruling 3 completes the table.*

### Ruling 2 — The Confederate thaw goes all the way, and the south becomes the one place the map can consolidate peacefully

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"All the way."* — answering how far ruling 1's thaw reaches.

**A Confederate claimant is an ordinary neighbour.** Two of them may warm up, sign, ally, federate or
unite while both still claim the prize. **The contest puts no ceiling on the relationship at all** —
it is a shared ambition, not a barrier.

**And one absorbing another is simply how the contest gets won.** Round 1's S66 already says whoever
holds most of the core takes the prize; ruling 2 makes *merging* a legitimate route to that, alongside
conquering. **So the Confederacy can be restored by agreement**, which is the only one of the four
contests that can.

**What this does to the board, and it is the point of the ruling.** The two western contests are
succession wars with no exit but force. **The south is the opposite: five nations who all want the
same thing and are allowed to talk about it.** Three genuinely different games on one map —
*the west fights, the south negotiates, and the north-east is still to be ruled.*

**Rejected, and both were put to him:** a truce ceiling, which would have let the five trade without
ever joining; and allying forfeiting the claim, which would have narrowed the contest each time two
got close. *Aaron took the version with no penalty attached, so the Confederacy is the cheap,
friendly corner of a hostile map.*

**⚠ It also makes the New Confederacy the largest peaceful prize in the game and nobody has priced
that.** Round 1 measured its homeland at **537 Areas** — the eleven Confederate states — and ruled it
*"cannot be done without Texas, which is five nations away."* Under ruling 2 the five southern
claimants can federate their way to most of it without a shot. **Whether the Texas requirement still
bites hard enough to stop that is a real question and it is not this round's** — it belongs with
whoever builds the contest. *Recorded as finding F.*

### Ruling 3 — The capital contest is permanent. Twenty-three pairs never thaw; ten do

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"Permanent, like Texas and California."* — completing ruling 1's table.

**Washington D.C., the United States of New England, Philadelphia and New York City are locked
Hostile all game**, on three pairs, and no thaw reaches them. The prize is the capital, and the
capital is the one thing that genuinely cannot be shared.

**The arithmetic, restated so nobody rebuilds it from the wrong number:**

| | Pairs | Thaw |
|---|---|---|
| A Free Texas | 10 | **Never** |
| California Republic | 10 | **Never** |
| The Thirteen Colonies | 3 | **Never** |
| The Confederacy | 10 | **Freely, ruling 2** |
| **Total** | **33** | **10 of 33 have a diplomatic exit; 23 do not** |

**⚠ The reason this is NOT arbitrary, and it is worth writing down because it looks arbitrary.**
Ruling 2's argument was that the Confederacy thaws because *it was never one country in living
memory* — five distinct regional nations who would have to agree to become something. **That argument
does not extend to the north-east**, and Aaron has drawn the line where it actually falls: D.C., New
England, Philadelphia and New York are not five strangers with a shared ambition. **They are four
pieces of a country that collapsed two years ago, three of which are the government's own
successors** — which is precisely the Texas situation with skyscrapers on it.

**So the rule underneath all three rulings is: a contest thaws when the thing being restored is
history, and stays frozen when it is last year.** *The Confederacy is a memory. Texas, California and
the Union are a bereavement.*

**What it does to the board.** **The richest, densest ground in the game becomes as ungovernable by
diplomacy as Texas.** Three of the largest city-states on the map can never sign anything with one
another, which means the eastern seaboard is a pressure cooker rather than a negotiation — *and the
south is now the only large region on the board where nations can freely combine.* **That asymmetry
is the shape of the map this round has just drawn**, and the alpha should be watched to see whether
the south runs away with the continent because of it.

### Ruling 4 — There are two multilateral objects. A bloc is light; a federation is heavy

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"Two objects — a bloc is light."* — answering spine question 4, and
in-tray items 3, k, l and u.

**A BLOC is a standing multilateral deal and nothing more.** Free movement of goods between members,
a common front toward everyone else, and no government of any kind: **no elected leader, no budget,
no turn of its own, no mutual defence.** That is what seven farm governors signing a piece of paper
actually is, and the story is explicit that the Farmers Union is *"not a state."*

**A FEDERATION is what round 3 ruled** — leader, budget, turn, flat toll, and an attack on one being
a war with all. **It is the heavy commitment, and the two are different objects with different
prices.**

| | Bloc | Federation |
|---|---|---|
| Leader | none | **elected**, ruling 25 |
| Budget and a turn of its own | none | **yes**, ruling 25 |
| Mutual defence | **none** | **an attack on one is a war with all**, ruling 28 |
| Internal trade | **free movement of goods** | **flat 10% toll replacing members' own arrangements**, rulings 25–26 |
| Peace | members may still quarrel | **at peace with every member, full stop**, ruling 30 |
| What it is in the story | the Farmers Union; Washington–Oregon | the United States of New England |

**Why this is right and the cheap answer was wrong.** Collapsing them would have meant seven farm
governors signing a trade pact and automatically acquiring an elected president and a joint army.
**The story has three light agreements and one heavy one**, and one object could only have described
one of them.

**Four defaults taken without asking, because each has a precedent already ruled.** *Say so if any is
wrong; none is a product judgement.*

1. **Three is the floor for a bloc too.** Ruling 34 set it for federations. Two nations agreeing
   something is a bilateral deal and the game already has those.
2. **Members vote you in; anyone may walk out.** Ruling 34's joining and leaving, reused wholesale.
3. **A bloc cannot expel.** Matching ruling 34, which gives federations no expulsion either. *T39
   argued for it; the default is the precedent, and it is the reversible choice.*
4. **A bloc does not make its members peaceful with each other.** That is ruling 30 and it is a
   federation's property. **Two members of the same bloc may be Hostile** — which is exactly Chicago
   leaving the Farmers Union with north-west Indiana in its pocket.

**⚠ And ruling 4 answers something round 3 asked and could not close.** Politics found that *"join a
bloc"* is the **concede-less answer to a Unify movement** — integration without a merger. That only
works if a bloc is cheap. **Under a one-object rule the concession would have been a federation**,
which is very nearly the merger the movement wanted, and the valve would have closed. *The light bloc
is what keeps round 3's ruling honest.*

**Still open, and it is the collision round 3 flagged:** the federation's flat, automatic,
unrefusable 10% against the build's negotiated, per-mode, per-direction, compounding corridors with a
notice period. **Ruling 4 makes it worse, not better** — there are now two internal-trade regimes to
reconcile with the built one instead of one. *T41, and it is the design stage's.*

### Ruling 5 — A vassal keeps its turn and loses its foreign policy. Conquest's Subject is closed

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"Foreign policy only."* — answering spine question 5, in-tray items 2,
(e) and (m), and **closing C112**, which conquest deferred here on 9 September.

**The vassal keeps its action and runs itself at home.** What it loses is the foreign column: **it
cannot sign a deal, grant a corridor, join a bloc, ally, or declare a war without its overlord's
consent.** The overlord holds a **veto over anything the vassal signs**. Beyond that: **a tithe of
income**, and **free passage for the overlord's goods.**

**Why this is the answer and the literal one was not.** C112 stated the problem exactly: *"A subject
that keeps its action is not subject; one that loses it leaves a player with no game to play."*
Ruling 5 refuses the second horn. **A vassal that keeps its turn still has a game** — it governs, it
builds, it manages its own movements, it can try to get strong enough to walk away — **and what it
has lost is the thing it would most want back**, which is the definition of a humiliation worth
playing through.

**So vassalage is playable from both sides, and that is what closes C112.** Conquest could not
resolve Subject because both answers were unacceptable; the third one — *lose a column, not the
turn* — was not on the table. **Annex takes ground, Unite absorbs a nation, and Vassal takes the
foreign policy.** The awkward middle now has a shape.

**And it sits exactly on top of what is already built.** §3e: paying aid already makes a nation a
client whose politics drift toward the donor's, one patron at a time, outbid-able and decaying.
**Ruling 5 is the formal layer over that informal one** — money buys drift, and vassalage buys the
veto. *Two tiers of influence where the game already had one, and the lower tier needs no work.*

**Three defaults taken, each with a precedent or the story behind it.** *Say so if any is wrong.*

1. **Vassalage can be demanded, and refusing is a cause for war.** The story rules this already:
   Houston demanded it of Louisiana, was refused, and invaded. **So it is T25's ultimatum pointed at
   sovereignty**, and it is how the story's largest war started.
2. **It can also be offered upward** — a small nation that cannot survive alone asks a large one to
   take it. T51. *The more interesting half, because the player is the one deciding what they are
   prepared to stop being.*
3. **A vassal may repudiate, and repudiating is a hostile act.** Not a free exit like leaving a bloc,
   or vassalage would mean nothing. The precedent is ruling 29 — *declare anyway, and you are out* —
   with the consequence landing on the vassal rather than the federation.

**Still open and not this round's:** what the overlord's veto costs the overlord to exercise, and
whether a vassal's tithe is a share of income or a fixed figure. **Both are numbers and both go to
the mechanics stage.**

### Ruling 6 — The petition line is a fixed gap below the secession line. Secession's finding A is closed

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"A fixed gap, set once."* — closing **secession's finding A**, which
politics ruling 12 declared blocking on 11 September, and answering spine question 2 and in-tray
item 10.

**One number, below the declaration threshold, the same for every movement in every nation.** A
movement that crosses the lower line **asks**; a movement that crosses the upper line **may leave**.
The gap between them is a single dial, and it is the same dial everywhere.

**Why this matters more than it sounds.** Today the two lines are the same, which means **no movement
in this game has ever asked for anything — every one of them declares.** Round 1 named the
consequence: *"Cascadia would take Portland and Seattle rather than ask for them."* **Ruling 6 creates
the middle act.** A movement spends a stretch of the game petitioning — of its own government, and of
the neighbours who could take it in — before independence is even available to it.

**And it gives the player warning they can act on.** The whole of round 3's table — concede, grant
self-rule, change course, join a bloc, send soldiers — **needs something to be answering.** Under one
threshold there is nothing to answer until it is too late to answer it.

**The number itself is the mechanics stage's**, per the standing rule that every model constant is a
named tunable. **What this round fixes is that it is ONE number and it is a gap, not a second
independent threshold.** *Rejected, and both were put to him: a gap that moves with the government's
record, and one that moves with the region's condition. Aaron took the constant, which is the version
that can be tuned after the alpha rather than argued about before it.*

**⚠ The game now has THREE thresholds on the same 0–1 share scale, and nobody has laid them beside
each other.** Written down here so the mechanics stage sets them as a set rather than one at a time:

| Line | What crossing it means | Constraint on record |
|---|---|---|
