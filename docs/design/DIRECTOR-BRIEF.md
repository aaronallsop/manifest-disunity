# Director's brief

**24 September 2026. ✅ APPROVED by Aaron the same day (D260).** Every role reads this first, and all of it. It holds Aaron's taste in his own words, so work is
checked against *him*, not the average strategy game. From today's interview and 639 checked places in older records.

**How to read it.** Quoted words with a source are his, word for word ([sic] marks his spelling), unless credited to
another record. Bold summaries, *Reading* and *Check* are the studio's and always give way to him. A review applies
every check the work touches, and names any it skips. **Precedence:** his latest word wins, even over this brief. On
taste, this brief beats older records (section 8); on what the built game does, the behaviour document rules. **Words:** "there's a lot of terminology that I end up using while chatting interchangeably, and that
gets really confusing for the programmers, because they think I say one thing but mean something else." (interview)
Until a terminology list exists, a word of his with two meanings is a question, not a guess.

## 1. What the game is for: five pillars

### Pillar 1: Looks complicated, plays easy
> "It looks really complicated at first, but once you get going, it's easy to understand." (Q1) · "at the end of it,
> there should be a very simple thing on the end." · "you can work with them on a deeper level, but you can also just
> play the game." (Q12, the second of his camera)

*Reading:* every system ends in one plain figure, word or state a newcomer grasps without the detail, like his "low
civil liberties" (Q12). The depth is there for whoever goes looking.
*Check:* can a first-time player read a system's end result and "know whether or not you're winning" (Q12, the 4DX
scoreboard)? Is what a newcomer does not need kept off the sidebar that "takes forever to scroll through" (Q13)?

### Pillar 2: A what-if you play with: a hard start, a long payoff, several goals at once
> "it was a lot of fun to play around with this alternate history." (Q1) · "a nation that on one hand has a potential
> advantage but right now is kind of stuck on all sides." (Q4, of EU4) · "trying to get a movement to rise up" · "you
> were the underdog, you worked really hard over small turns to get to something and then you got a big payout that paid
> off." (Q2) · "there's always multiple goals that you're working at accomplishing at one time." (Q4, of Civ 4)

*Reading:* a start is a puzzle: something in its favour, pressure on every side. Patient work, such as nursing a
movement over many turns, pays off big. Several goals run at once, including ones the game sets.
*Check* (until he rules on how hard a start may be): told the start in a sentence, does an outsider hear "a fun
challenge" or "a terrible nation to start with" (Q14)? Can patient small moves win a small nation a big gain (Q2)?

### Pillar 3: You learn something real without meaning to
> "I also realized that the state of Jefferson is actually a real thing that people have proposed" (Q1) · "I do want to
> have the history built into it." (Q7) · "like geography, play such a huge importance in our world" (Q4, Zeihan)

*Reading:* the real thing arrives through play (the map, missions, events, the newspaper), not as explanation on a
panel. Geography decides outcomes, which is why the game has waterways (Q4).
*Check:* does each nation, mission and event carry one real thing a curious player could look up and find true? For
systems work, only: does geography change the outcome, or is it scenery?

### Pillar 4: A loss is never a cheat, and it teaches you something
> "I don't want them to feel cheated or robbed in any way. I think that they need to feel like it happened because of a
> certain thing that happened." · "I know what I did wrong and I know what I need to do differently next time, when I
> start this game up." (Q5) · "It is only a good story if at the end of it, they have learned something that they can do
> differently and do next turn." (Q14) *He confirmed the same day: "next turn" means **the next sixty-round game they
> play** — the same lesson as Q5.*

*Reading:* a loss traces to something the player could see: a risk that did not pay off, growth that did not come (Q5).
Shown chance is fine: "it feels like your generals analysed the data and are giving you a chance to win / succeed"
(conquest ruling 12, 7 Sep). This covers the player's own fortunes; his Q2 surprise says not all is explained up front.
*Check:* after a loss, can a tester who did not build it say what went wrong and one thing to do differently? Can a
player find, in the game, the cause of a change to their own nation (ground lost or gained, a movement rising, a
government falling)?

### Pillar 5: Systems, not stereotypes, and the absurd played straight
> "I don't want stereotype in the mechanics. I do want there to be satire, a little bit of farce, overt characterization
> in the content." · "the complexity of these intersecting systems to tell a story that is unique and not just a
> flattening of that" (Q3) · "wait a second. How did Minnesota take over New York City?" · "the feeling of absurdity
> that is hilarious" · "it's just this weird, hilarious alternate universe" (Q2) · "it is these ridiculous things, but
> they treat it in universe as serious." (Q4, of Fallout) · "a really sharp sardonic voice" (Q1)

*Reading:* a *label* is a political or cultural identity (party, ideology, movement, religion, regional reputation) with
no built-in reward or penalty; what a nation holds, how it is governed and who leads it are not labels (Q8). Absurd
outcomes come from systems meeting, not a script; the world treats them as serious. The voice comes in the beta.
*Check:* is any outcome decided by a stereotype of the label alone, like "if you are a communist country you have less
food" (Q3)? His libertarian nation with no welfare, because its churches do that work, passes (Q3). Characterisation
stays in the words, never in a rule. Does the piece leave room for an improbable chain a player would retell?

## 2. The tie-break: when simple and story pull apart (Q12)

> "there should be two options weighted. One is: can the history be adjusted — or the reality, not just the history —
> adjusted in a way to create a simulacrum of feasibility. And then another one would be: will focusing on a certain
> thing lower too much the amount of fun the average viewer would have." · "at the end of the day, the point of this is
> to be fun." · "the simplicity of the game needs to be in learning it" (Q12)

*Reading:* weigh the two tests together; neither comes first. Simplicity lives in learning the game, not in the engine,
so prefer moving depth out of sight. Cutting is his call, and he has cut for the alpha before: "for the first real alpha
we want this to still be a simple game" (economy ruling 2, 14 Sep). Other clashes go to him.

## 3. Who the player is (Q8)

> "the kings or queen or whatever your leader is are constantly changing, and that impacts how well you can do things.
> And so I like the idea of it being: you are the nation itself that you are playing."

*Reading:* governments and leaders change underneath; the player is the nation throughout.

## 4. What the alpha must prove, and what waits for the beta

**Proposed by the studio and approved with the brief (D260):** a checklist for the stage-3 alpha (D248; Texas, the Great Lakes and the
West, D239) to replace the roster's unattributed alpha goal. Until then the project's definition of done stands (section
8, item 3), and the Economy-mode alpha test (v0.6) is untouched. A new tester plays sixty turns and:

1. can play it at all: "get the alpha up and running and able to be played at least." (Q6)
2. finds it complicated-looking but easy once going (pillar 1), with something worth doing every turn (section 7).
3. can say what went wrong and one thing to do differently (pillar 4).
4. meets some real history in play: "there is going to be some history, but not a lot. I do want to have the history
   built into it." (Q7; confirmed 24 Sep: **the alpha gets some history**, D261)
5. when asked, wants to "experiment and play with different play styles and nations" (Q1).

**The voice waits for the beta:** "the beta version is when I would definitely want that kind of sharp, biting, sardonic
voice to show up." (Q6) **The look stays:** "for the alpha, how the game is — the overall look is fine", the map like
"an interactive map you would see on a website for an article". **The interface comes first, timing his:** "not so much
the art of the design, but the user interface and experience. Because right now this is a huge problem" (Q13).

## 6. What the game refuses to be

**Three acts are never a move:** forcing a population out, mass killing, and nuclear weapons against cities (tone rule
34). He confirmed them provisionally: "I think right now, those do stay." (Q9) Conquest ruling 31 (9 Sep) goes further:
no nuclear weapons at all. The ruling is his, its wording the studio's; rule 34 does not narrow it. *Reading:* the acts
may be named as history, or as one paper's accusation (rule 34's allowance stands), never as a button. Why:
> "the fact of the matter is that American history is bloody. There's a lot of blood on America's hands in our history.
> And I don't want to ignore that, or pretend that didn't happen, in any way. But I also want to be sensitive, and
> realize that although this can be a game about history, that helps people learn, it is still a video game." · "this is
> still a realistic game set in some reality, but it's still a fantasy for people to escape to. I don't want to have a
> mechanic where Christian nationalism rises up and it's giving the player a choice to expel their LGBTQ minority,
> because that seems dark and not something I would want to hard code as a possibility." (Q9)

**The Dan Olson lesson.** "with Minecraft, that was a system that was not intentional. But with this game, because the
backdrop is America and an alternate history, something like that is no longer funny" (Q9). *Reading:* joined systems
could do by accident what the refusals forbid; whether he means that as a rule is open. *Check:* can a combination of
systems do it by the back door? People choosing to leave is allowed; the player moving people out is not.

**Persecuting a group is never a move; it shows only as civil liberties falling.** "that's where just the term 'civil
liberties would go down' would be a blanket catch-all." (Q9) Asked whether people choosing to leave such a nation is on
the right side of the line: "I think that is more or less the right line. And we would get into more specifics later" ·
"it would impact people less if you were the matching movement." (Q9 follow-up; the design already does this)

**Military is something a nation has, never pieces you move.** "I do want to stand firm on not having military units, in
the sense that they are a unit that you can move and direct and interact with as a separate entity outside of the map.
You will have military, and I think in later versions — and I have talked about this — where, oh, Hawaii has Pearl
Harbor, so they have a good navy or something. That is something I do want. But it isn't like you were going to say, I
need to move my battleships here." (Q10) Earlier: "There isn't going to be troops / troop types / etc." (conquest ruling
11, 7 Sep) *Reading:* Hawaii's navy is ruling 31's naval base.

**For:** "someone who like me [sic]" (D232). **Not for:** those who would call it "woke" ("I'm not really targeting
them", Q10); those who want units, "a form of mismarketing and positioning rather than game" (Q10); those finding it
"way too complex": "this also isn't for them" (Q12).

**The jokes' lines** are parked to the beta (Q11). Until then his earlier lines stand: "I wouldn't want to make a joke
about death like that." (tone rule 20) · "the joke is at why there is a famine." (rule 22) · "where I would draw the
line is when the jokes touch on racial topics" (D238, 15 Sep; never amended) · indigenous nations' missions "can include
dignified allusions and jokes if they are not at their expense." (D242, 16 Sep)

## 7. The failure we fear most (Q10, Q3)

> "it's cool and it's kind of funny to see these things, but there just isn't much to do. From a gameplay perspective,
> it's just boring." (Q10)

A competent build can miss in two ways (Q3). **The calculator:** "this is just a calculator with a graphical design
interface. And it is essentially just a simulation." **The cartoon:** "there isn't a lot of history to it … just relying
on shallow stereotypes". *Check:* in an hour of play, what did the player decide each turn? A turn whose best move is
simply to end it is the failure itself. That tests the calculator; pillars 3 and 5 test the cartoon.
