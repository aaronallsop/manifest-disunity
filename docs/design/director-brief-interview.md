# Director's brief — the interview, as it happened

**Started 24 September 2026, in a Claude Code session on the MacBook.** Aaron answers by voice; his
answers are recorded here in his words, with only filler ("um", "uh") and false starts removed. Nothing
here is paraphrase. The brief itself is written from this file, and where the two ever disagree, this
file is what he actually said.

**Method.** The questions are asked cold first: before he is shown anything he said in earlier weeks.
A background sweep of the project's records gathers what he said before. Afterwards the two are laid
side by side: where they agree, that is a pillar; where they differ, today's answer is the rule and the
older one is marked superseded, once, with his confirmation.

---

## Round 1 — what the game is for

### Q1. A playtester finishes sixty turns and texts a friend. What do you want them to say it's like?

> I found this really cool game. It looks really complicated at first, but once you get going, it's
> easy to understand. And it's a lot of fun. I was able to simulate what would happen if Texas seceded.
> And it was a lot of fun to play around with this alternate history. It was also really cool because
> it had a really sharp sardonic voice that was really funny, and at the same time I also realized that
> the state of Jefferson is actually a real thing that people have proposed — I didn't realize that that
> was actually something that was real. It is a little bit like Europa Universalis IV, but it's all in a
> map, which makes it easier to play on any device.
>
> And if we're looking at this through the context of the playtester who is playtesting the alpha, I
> would like them to be like: it was a lot of fun, but honestly, I could see it growing, and it sounds
> like a lot of the features they've got is going to make it a really fun game where you can kind of
> experiment and play with different play styles and nations.

### Q2. One specific moment you want every player to have, and what they feel.

> I think one moment that I would like every player to have in the game — and maybe there's like two of
> them. Maybe there's three. And you'll notice that this is just the way that my brain works, that I
> can't settle on one.
>
> First, one of them is they're playing the game and they're so focused on their part of the nation
> that they look over and they're like, wait a second. How did Minnesota take over New York City? Or
> wait a second. The Confederacy fell apart, but it rose again in the North. How did that happen? And
> the idea with that is that it's just these silly, ridiculous things that are happening that if you're
> not paying attention, you're like, wait, how did that happen?
>
> The second thing that I want people to feel is the feeling of accomplishment when they've been
> trying to get a movement to rise up. And it's one of those things where it finally does and they go
> from being this super small underdog nation to taking over most of another nation or something —
> just this idea of like you were the underdog, you worked really hard over small turns to get to
> something and then you got a big payout that paid off.
>
> I think the third thing that I would want people to experience is the feeling of absurdity that is
> hilarious. I don't know exactly what it would be as an example in my game because I haven't got
> there yet. But the one example I use a lot that I keep going back to is the world record for fastest
> world conquest in EU4 — and to be clear, it isn't the fastest that somebody did that in real time,
> it's the fastest they did it in in-game time — was that they conquered the world as Mongolia, who was
> the emperor of the HRE. And so it's just this weird, hilarious alternate universe where it's like,
> okay, yeah, what if Oirat decided to become the Mongolian Empire again and push so far west,
> converted to Christianity, and became the HRE and took over the world. So I would say those
> experiences are what people would want.

### Q3. Someone else builds it from the same documents. It's competent, but it isn't yours. What's wrong with it?

> I think if that were to happen, there would be kind of two things, on either end of the spectrum. One
> would be somebody builds it and I play it, I'm like, this is just a calculator with a graphical design
> interface. And it is essentially just a simulation. And so there isn't much fun to it.
>
> The other end of the spectrum where somebody implements it is they play it and they're like, this is
> a lot of fun, but there isn't a lot of history to it and it's very unrealistic in a sense — in the
> sense that it's not really based on history, or it's very blunt with it and simplistic, where it is
> like, oh, communism: people are hungry because there's no food to eat in communism; or oh, there are
> Republicans — just relying on shallow stereotypes and not allowing for the complexity of these
> intersecting systems to tell a story that is unique and not just a flattening of that.
>
> Because I think one thing I see in video games sometimes — which for the games that they're making,
> maybe that is the right choice — is the very stereotypical gameplay mechanics. Whereas with this, I
> don't want stereotype in the mechanics. I do want there to be satire, a little bit of farce, overt
> characterization in the content. But I don't want it just to be this super simple, like, oh, if you
> are a communist country you have less food; if you are a libertarian country you have zero welfare
> support.
>
> Because I could see a nation being a hundred percent libertarian, and despite not having any social
> welfare programs, the society is also built around something else. Maybe it's got a very large church
> culture, or religious organizations, and they are so integral to that society's cultural identity that
> the idea of a social welfare program actually wouldn't make sense — because it's like, well, why would
> the government do it when the church is not only doing it, but they actually do it. So those are kind
> of my thoughts.

### Q4. Outside games — or in them — what already has the feeling you're after, and what exactly would you steal?

*His first attempt was cut off mid-sentence by the voice input; this is the full second answer.*

> Europa Universalis, like I was saying — that is a game that I think I draw a lot of inspiration from.
> And that kind of feeling of like: you've got this challenge, you've got a unique situation, you've got
> a nation that on one hand has a potential advantage but right now is kind of stuck on all sides. It's
> that idea of like, you start in EU4, but you've got to deal with France, which is a really powerful
> nation. Or you're right up against the Ottomans and you're like, how do I handle this?
>
> And another game, I think tone-wise, is Fallout 4 and the Fallout universe in general. I do like how
> ridiculous it can be while still remaining serious. And what I mean by that is, it is these
> ridiculous things, but they treat it in universe as serious. And they're definitely way more silly
> and dark than I would want to go. But that handling is something that I do really like.
>
> As far as films, shows, books, podcasts that I draw on — one of them is Peter Zion, I think his name
> is. I read his books, Accidental Superpower, and the other one I can't remember the name of. And I
> just really found it so fascinating: his view on how things that I would have never thought of, like
> geography, play such a huge importance in our world, which makes sense. And that should actually be
> in the game notes somewhere. He talks about how waterways are so important and what helped America
> grow, and that's kind of where I got this idea of having waterways as a part of the nation. Those are
> kind of the big three, I think, off the top of my mind right now.
>
> Civilization 2, I think, is another kind of big influence — especially their turn design, and how
> this isn't a real-time game like EU4 is. And I like how Civilization 4 — and I think I've talked about
> this with you or another Claude — just the incremental rewards, and how there's always multiple goals
> that you're working at accomplishing at one time. And so if you accomplish one, there's still other
> ones that you were working on.

## Round 2 — winning, losing and the voice

### Q5. Turn 60, a tester's nation has just been swallowed by a neighbour. What should they feel?

> I think the feeling that they should feel at turn 60 and they just got swallowed up by a neighbor is:
> I know what I need to do next time. I don't want them to feel cheated or robbed in any way. I think
> that they need to feel like it happened because of a certain thing that happened. Or, like, maybe
> they took a risk and it didn't pay off. Or they tried something and they didn't grow their country the
> way that they did, and they got swallowed up. But overall, I want them to end thinking: I know what I
> did wrong and I know what I need to do differently next time, when I start this game up.

*Note:* this confirms, in his own words, the master file's definition of "working" — *"tell you
afterwards why they lost"* — whose authorship the records did not show. It is now his.

### Q6. The friend praised the sardonic voice, but jokes and writing were pushed past the alpha. Does the alpha tester get the voice?

> I pushed them past the alpha because at the time I really just wanted to get to the alpha, and it was
> going really, really slow with the way that I was doing it. And now I'm seeing that that is in part due
> to the inefficiency of the way that I had Claude Code and the agentic system set up. I think for the
> alpha, I still do want to push that back a bit, because I really do want to get the alpha up and
> running and able to be played at least. I think the beta version is when I would definitely want that
> kind of sharp, biting, sardonic voice to show up.

*Note:* this **confirms** D242 (the writing goes post-alpha) rather than superseding it, and adds the
target: **the voice arrives in the beta.** The voice is a pillar of the finished game that the alpha
deliberately does not test. The "one line that made the friend laugh" part was not answered — a beta
question.

### Q7. "Any historical context" was also pushed past the alpha. Is real history a beta thing too?

> You are right. And so I think that there is going to be some history, but not a lot. I do want to have
> the history built into it.

*Note — this SUPERSEDES part of D242.* On 16 September he ruled: *"And lets also push any historical
context to be incldued post-alpha as well."* (`DECISIONS.md` 6038). Today: **the alpha carries some
history, not a lot, built into the game.** The jokes-and-writing half of D242 stands (Q6). To be
recorded as a dated superseding entry in `DECISIONS.md` when the brief is written.

### Q8. Who is the player — a president, a government, a party, or the nation itself?

> I think the player is very much like EU4, in the sense that you are technically the leader of the
> nation, but it isn't like you are the very specific leader that is currently in charge. As in EU4, the
> kings or queen or whatever your leader is are constantly changing, and that impacts how well you can
> do things. And so I like the idea of it being: you are the nation itself that you are playing.

*Note:* fills a gap the records named outright — `GDD.md` §4 said no statement of the player's role
existed. **The player is the nation. Leaders come and go, and who is in charge changes how well the
nation can do things.**

## Round 3 — what the game refuses to be

### Q9. Three things are never a button: forcing a population out, mass killing, nuclear weapons against cities. Do they stand, and why?

> I think right now, those do stay. I think there might have been a parsing error. And maybe we can just
> talk about this, and we can take what I say here and override that.
>
> The main thing is I want to draw on history, and the fact of the matter is that American history is
> bloody. There's a lot of blood on America's hands in our history. And I don't want to ignore that, or
> pretend that didn't happen, in any way. But I also want to be sensitive, and realize that although
> this can be a game about history, that helps people learn, it is still a video game.
>
> And so with getting people out, and expulsion, the main thing that I was thinking about is making
> sure that this is still a realistic game set in some reality, but it's still a fantasy for people to
> escape to. I don't want to have a mechanic where Christian nationalism rises up and it's giving the
> player a choice to expel their LGBTQ minority, because that seems dark and not something I would want
> to hard code as a possibility. And so for that, I think that's where just the term "civil liberties
> would go down" would be a blanket catch-all.
>
> I'm reminded of this YouTube video I saw by the YouTuber Dan Olson, about how he created a Minecraft
> server and he was testing out this new mechanic where if you had a village it would grow with other
> people in Minecraft. But he didn't want to always go down to this village; it was on the other side of
> the map. And so what he did was he created a system where he would push these villagers onto a boat,
> bring that boat to the other side of where his area was, and had them build a village there. And he
> talked about how, although this wasn't an intentional outcome of the game, essentially the mechanic
> that was built into the game was mirroring that of colonialism and transatlantic slavery. And the
> video was an interesting talk about how video game systems play out in that way.
>
> And I think the difference here is that with Minecraft, that was a system that was not intentional.
> But with this game, because the backdrop is America and an alternate history, something like that is
> no longer funny in the sense of "oh gosh, that is ridiculous, I can't believe that is something that
> you can do" — because it is no longer in this realm of fantasy like Minecraft is, because it is still
> based in the contextual history of America. So that's, I think, kind of the main thing. Does that make
> sense?

*Note:* the three refusals in `TONE.md` rule 34 **stand**, and now carry his reasons in his words. He
explicitly asked for this answer to override the tone document's wording where they differ. New
lookbook entry: **Dan Olson's video about his Minecraft villager mechanic** — watched by him.

### Q10. Someone quits after an hour and writes a one-star review. What were they hoping for that this game will never give them?

> I think there's two ways for me to conceptualize this one-star review. The first is what they would say
> that would make me be like, oh gosh, I failed in implementing the game. And the other would be, I
> failed in marketing the game.
>
> If I failed in implementing the game, I think the one-star review would be like: it's cool, but it's
> just not fun. After an hour, they're like, yeah, it's cool and it's kind of funny to see these things,
> but there just isn't much to do. From a gameplay perspective, it's just boring. I think that's the main
> concern from that point.
>
> From a mismarketing side of things, I think there's two things. One would be somebody leaving the game
> and being like, oh, this game is woke or whatever. And that's just because video gamers are that way —
> or anything that doesn't follow their myopic view of the world. And not all video gamers, obviously.
> And I wouldn't want that, because I'm not really targeting them.
>
> And another thing would be somebody leaves a one-star review and they're like: there's no military
> function, you take over a country by just saying "take over", and that is no fun. And that I think is
> a bigger thing, because I do want to stand firm on not having military units, in the sense that they
> are a unit that you can move and direct and interact with as a separate entity outside of the map. You
> will have military, and I think in later versions — and I have talked about this — where, oh, Hawaii
> has Pearl Harbor, so they have a good navy or something. That is something I do want. But it isn't
> like you are going to say, I need to move my battleships here. And that would be a form of
> mismarketing and positioning rather than game.

*Note:* **the failure he fears is the implementation one: "it's cool, but there isn't much to do."**
The two marketing ones become the "not for" list, now in his words: players who want the game to share
their "myopic view of the world", and players who want units to move. **No movable units** is restated
as a firm refusal: military is a capability a nation has because of what it holds (Hawaii's navy from
Pearl Harbor), never a piece the player directs.

*Not yet answered:* whether people leaving a nation whose civil liberties fall (migration) is on the
right side of the Q9 line.

### Q9, follow-up. People choosing to leave a nation whose civil liberties fall — right side of the line?

> I think that is more or less the right line. And we would get into more specifics later, right? Where
> civil liberties would impact people, but it would impact people less if you were the matching
> movement. So if a Christian nationalist nation had low civil liberties and they conquered another
> nation, people who are already Christian nationalists would not be as impacted by that low civil
> liberty, because either A, those are the rules that they would want in life, or B, they are not the
> targets of the things that are causing the low civil liberties.

*Checked the same day:* the design already works this way — **liberty satisfaction is derived from the
political distance between the people and their government** (`GDD.md` 763, `identity-design.md` 142),
so low liberties hurt those who do not share the government's politics and barely touch those who do.

## Round 4 — when the pillars collide

### Q12. When keeping it simple and making a better story pull apart, which wins?

> That's a good one. I think when they clash, there should be two options weighted. One is: can the
> history be adjusted — or the reality, not just the history — adjusted in a way to create a simulacrum
> of feasibility. And then another one would be: will focusing on a certain thing lower too much the
> amount of fun the average player would have.
>
> And at the end of the day, the point of this is to be fun. And to me, keeping it simple and making a
> better story, those two work together to make it fun. This could potentially be the kind of game that
> is way too complex for people, that people won't enjoy — but this also isn't for them. And so for me,
> the simplicity of the game needs to be in learning it and quickly understanding how things work. If
> somebody who has never played the game before started as a nation and they saw low civil liberties,
> they would intuitively understand what that means. And that would be simple. Even if the exact formula
> to get the civil liberties figured out under the hood, as well as all the things that influence it, is
> a little more complicated — at the end of it, there should be a very simple thing on the end. I think
> that is what I'm going for.
>
> It reminds me of the book 4DX, where he talks about the four disciplines of execution. And one of the
> things he talks about is how you need to have a scoreboard. And a scoreboard for your goals needs to be
> clear and easy to read, so that you can just look at it and you know whether or not you're winning.
> How that can apply to this: there's going to be a lot of complex, intricate systems moving back and
> forth. But at the end of the day, people need to be able to quickly look at these things, see how they
> interact on a basic level, and know how they work, so they can have fun without needing to get into
> the details.
>
> I think about my camera. I have a Fuji X-T3, and this is a pretty common thing among all modern-day
> mirrorless cameras: they have an auto mode and they have a manual mode. I prefer using the manual mode
> because that's just how I learned photography, but also with film. So my camera is always in manual
> mode. And I have created custom Fuji looks, specifically based on film profiles that I have wanted to
> emulate. I have controlled all of these things, and it's been a great camera. At the same time,
> somebody could still put the camera in auto mode, select from one of the many Fuji looks that they
> have, like Provia, and still really enjoy the camera and take a good picture. And it's not like we're
> using a different camera or a different system. It's that the systems we are using are at different
> levels: you can work with them on a deeper level, but you can also just play the game.

*Note:* he named the book as "4DX by Mr. Cubby" — *The 4 Disciplines of Execution* (McChesney, Covey
and Huling); the scoreboard is its third discipline. New lookbook entries, both his own: **4DX's
scoreboard** and **his Fuji X-T3's auto and manual modes**.

### Q13. Picture the map screen. What does it look like?

> I think looking at the map screen, I would think it looks more like an interactive map you would see on
> a website for an article about "here's this thing that's happening". I think right now, for the alpha,
> how the game is — the overall look is fine. I do want to at a future point work on the design, and I
> think the main thing is not so much the art of the design, but the user interface and experience.
> Because right now this is a huge problem that we haven't gotten to, because it's been on the actual
> game design. The sidebar in which you control everything takes forever to scroll through, and shows
> you all this information, which is great, but when you don't need it, it's useless. So that's kind of
> my thought.

*Note:* the look is **an interactive map from a news article**. The alpha's look stays as it is. **The
priority for later is the interface, not the art**, and he names a live problem in today's build: **the
control sidebar shows everything at once and takes forever to scroll.** It is the same principle as Q12
— a plain figure up front, the detail when you ask for it — and it is evidence for when the roster's
deferred UX Designer role gets hired.

### Q14. A new player's nation is finished by turn 10 because of where it sits. Good story or bug? What should the nation-picker have told them?

> It is only a good story if at the end of it, they have learned something that they can do differently
> next time. I think one of the things about these games that I like is that when people hear about
> that, they don't think, oh, that sounds like a terrible nation to start with. They say, that sounds
> like a fun challenge. And so I feel like part of that will just be the natural outflow built into it.
>
> But if I'm looking at this as an actual market game that I could sell, I think I would very much want
> to approach it via the EU4 model, where I would release the alpha and beta with a few actual missions,
> and then there would be a DLC where it's like, oh okay, here is this nation's mission that you could
> try.

*Note:* **a doomed start is a good story only if the player learns what to do differently** — the same
test as Q5. The reaction to aim for when someone hears about a hard nation is *"that sounds like a fun
challenge"*, not *"that sounds like a terrible start."* The nation-picker part was answered only by
that. **New and commercial:** the EU4 model — the alpha and beta ship with a few mission trees, and
further nations' trees come later as paid additions. Recorded as his current thinking, not a ruling.
