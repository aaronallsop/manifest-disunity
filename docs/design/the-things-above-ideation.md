## 3. What is actually built today — verified 14 September 2026

### 3a. ⚠ The one-action rule is not a rule. It is the shape of the turn itself

**This is the most important thing in the round and nobody had written it down.**

**A "turn" in this game is a ROUND**, and a round is **one pass through all sixty-one nations, in a
shuffled order.** Each nation gets its own slot; taking its action ends its slot and passes the
pointer to the next. **When the pointer wraps, the world advances once** — every model phase, from
political drift through migration and growth, runs at that moment and not before.

**So one turn = one quarter = sixty-one nation-actions, resolved in sequence, followed by one
simulation step.**

**There is no "has acted this turn" flag anywhere, because there does not need to be one.** *Your turn
IS your action.* **Which means A1 — "change the whole one action per turn" — is not a rule change. It
is a change to the turn structure**, and every estimate of what it costs has to start there.

**One measured figure the code records, quoted rather than re-measured:** an AI round is **735 plans
and 153 ms**. *A structure that gives every nation two actions a quarter is two passes, so roughly
twice that — which is affordable, and it is not the reason to hesitate.*

### 3b. Eleven moves exist, and exactly one of them is already free

The planners: **annex, unite, release, govern, autonomy, recognise, trade, treaty, aid, transit,
revoke.** **Recognise costs no money and takes no action** — it is the precedent in-tray item 6 is
pointing at, and it has been free since before any of these rounds ran.

**Politics ruling 12 created the second free thing** — answering a movement's demands — and made it
**mandatory as well**, which is a different and stronger claim than Recognise's.

### 3c. Three victory paths, and the conditions are data rather than code

| Path | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **Reunification of the Union** | share of the **seats of government** held, share of the **people**, share of the **economy**, plus floors under **Authority** and **Influence** |
| **Ideological Dominance** | share of the continent's people **holding your ideology**, plus floors under Authority and Influence |
| **Economic Supremacy** | share of the **economy**, **output per head against the median nation**, and **quality of life** |

**The design note in the engine is the thing round 1's in-tray item 2 is really asking about:** *"a
beloved hegemon reunifies through nations it never invaded"*, and **a big state starts closer to
reunifying the Union than a small one.** *So the answer to "does Washington get a different victory
from Dallas" is already partly yes — not by a different rule, but because the same rule is a different
distance away.*

### 3d. ⚠ There is exactly ONE piece of hidden information in the entire game

**Verified by searching for it: the pressure map, and nothing else.**

> *"FOG. Exact bands for your own ground; for everyone else's, only calm / rising / critical. You know
> the temperature of your own country and you read the newspaper about everyone else's — and the fog
> is what stops the pressure map from being an omniscient targeting overlay for the annex button."*

**Six bands for your own ground, three for everybody else's.** *That is the whole of information
control in this game.* **Every stock, every treasury, every relationship, every deal, every corridor
and every movement's exact strength is fully visible on every nation's panel.**

**And the one piece that exists was built for a reason worth generalising:** *not to create mystery,
but to stop a screen becoming a targeting computer.* **That is a far better test than "what would a
government plausibly know", and it is the test in-tray items 5 and 11 should probably be answered
against.**

*(`disclosure.js` looks like an information system and is not — it folds the panel so a newcomer meets
six lines instead of sixteen blocks. It hides nothing; everything is one click away.)*

### 3e. Time, as built

**Four turns to a year, opening 1 March 2036.** Agreement durations are **2 / 4 / 8 / 20 turns** — six
months to five years — ruled in D163. **Memory decays per turn** and the honeymoon of a new nation is
four turns. **The two years of backstory are eight turns of decay**, which is in-tray item 1's
question.

### 3f. The opening roster, restated

and are not built. **Programmer rule 17.**

## 5. The idea bank

*Numbered W1, W2, … Unjudged.*

### A. What replaces the one action

**W1 — Keep one action and let triage be the game.** Round 4's own answer: *"the real decision is
which of your problems to leave unsolved."* **The rule is not broken; it is the source of every
interesting decision in six rounds.** *Against Aaron's instruction, and recorded because the argument
is strong and nobody has written it down.*

**W2 — Two actions a quarter.** The smallest possible change: the pointer passes twice. **Costs about
twice the AI time — 735 plans and 153 ms becomes roughly double — which is affordable.** *And it
halves the pressure that made every round complain, without changing anything conceptually.*

**W3 — One action per CATEGORY.** A military action, a diplomatic action and a domestic action each
quarter. **Six components stop competing** because they are no longer in the same queue. *The obvious
objection: it removes the trade-off that made the budget interesting, and a nation that does not want
to fight loses nothing by attacking somebody.*

**W4 — Actions cost points from a budget, and different acts cost differently.** Annexing costs three,
recognising costs nothing, an overture costs one. **The budget is the thing round 7 hands out and
every other round prices against.** *This is the version that needs no further rulings from anybody —
each round's deferred "does it cost the action" becomes a number rather than a yes or no.*

**W5 — The budget grows with the nation.** A large, well-run state does more per quarter than a
collapsing one. **Authority already exists and already means roughly this.** *Makes the budget a stock
rather than a constant, and gives Authority a second job it is well suited to.*

**W6 — Against W5: a budget that grows with success is a snowball.** The nation that is winning acts
more, which is how it keeps winning.

**W7 — The budget shrinks under pressure.** War weariness, occupation and low authority all take
actions away. *The same idea as W5 pointed the other way, and it is the version that helps the
underdog rather than the leader.*

**W8 — Sub-turns: decide quarterly, execute monthly.** **F1, Aaron's own**, and D163 chose the
quarter deliberately as *its outer clock so nothing was foreclosed.* **The round structure already IS
a sequence inside a quarter**, so the machinery is closer than it looks.

**W9 — Free actions are unlimited and paid actions are one.** The cheapest reconciliation: keep the
single action for anything that changes the world, and let everything that merely *answers* be free.

**W10 — Some actions take more than one turn.** A campaign, a negotiation, a construction. **Nothing
in this game currently takes time**, and a multi-turn commitment is a different way to limit a player
than a per-turn cap.

**W11 — You may bank an unused action.** A quiet quarter buys a busy one. *Rewards patience and
punishes nothing.*

**W12 — The AI gets the same budget as the player, whatever it is.** *Stated because it has been true
so far and would be easy to lose.*

### B. What is free, and why

**W13 — A decision is free when you did not choose to be asked.** **The unifying principle behind
every exception so far**: a movement's demands arrive whether you like it or not (politics ruling 12),
an event arrives unbidden (round 6), and recognition is a standing question about somebody else's
existence rather than an act of your own. *Three exceptions, one rule, and nobody had noticed it.*

**W14 — A decision is free when refusing is also a decision.** A demand you can only answer by
answering. *Narrower than W13 and sharper.*

**W15 — Free decisions are mandatory.** Ruling 12 made them so: *answered before the turn can move.*
**A free decision you may ignore is a notification, not a decision.**

**W16 — Against W15: a mandatory free decision every turn from six components is a queue, not a
game.** *If every round gets ruling 12's channel, a player answers six cards before doing anything.
That is the real risk and it is why ruling 12 recorded itself as an exception.*

**W17 — A cap on free decisions.** Three a quarter, and the rest wait.

**W18 — Maintaining a standing arrangement is free; changing one costs.** **Item 6's answer in one
line.** A corridor you already granted runs itself; revoking it costs. A bloc you are in costs
nothing; joining or leaving costs. *Consistent with Recognise, and it means the story's world of
standing arrangements does not eat the turn budget.*

**W19 — Against W18: then a nation with many arrangements is strictly better off**, and nothing pushes
back on accumulating them.

**W20 — An arrangement has an upkeep that is not an action but is a cost.** Money, attention,
authority. *The pushback W19 asks for, without spending the budget.*

### C. What a nation may know

**W21 — The test is not plausibility, it is whether the screen becomes a targeting computer.** §3d's
own reason for the only fog in the game, generalised. **A far better rule than "what would a
government know", because it can be applied to a specific screen and answered.**

**W22 — Everything is visible, as today, and that is a choice not an oversight.** The game explains
every number it shows; hiding some would make the explanations lie. *And a strategy game where you
cannot see the board is a different genre.*

**W23 — Stocks are visible; intentions are not.** You can see a nation's treasury, army and unrest.
You cannot see what it is about to do. **That is already true and it is most of what fog is for.**

**W24 — What you can see depends on the relationship.** An ally's panel is open; a hostile nation's is
three bands and a guess. **Round 5 built the relationship vocabulary this would read**, and it would
give alliances a value nobody has priced.

**W25 — Against W24: it makes the AI's job much harder and the player's much fiddlier.** *Recorded
with it, because the two are the same idea seen from opposite sides.*

**W26 — A bloc you are not in is opaque; a bloc you are in is open.** In-tray item 5, answered in one
line. *And it gives ruling 4's light bloc a second reason to exist.*

**W27 — A vassal's overlord sees everything the vassal sees.** *Which is most of what being a vassal
means, and it costs nothing to say.*

**W28 — Stateless ground has nothing to know.** Round 5's ruling 16 made it terrain with a price —
**no government, no secrets.** *Item 5's third clause answers itself.*

**W29 — An offer is visible to everyone, or to nobody, and the board's atmosphere rests on it.** T26.
**Sponsorship is already ruled visible to all** (secession ruling 21), so there is a precedent
pointing one way.

**W30 — Information decays.** You saw their treasury last quarter; you are reading a number that is
three months old. **The memory list already works exactly like this** and nobody has pointed it at
facts rather than feelings.

**W31 — There is no fog, but there is NOISE.** Every figure about somebody else is shown as a band
rather than a number. *The pressure map already does this and it is the only precedent in the game.*

### D. Winning

**W32 — Three paths is right and the question is only whether they are reachable.** Built, tuned, and
nobody has finished a game to find out.

**W33 — Reunification means replacing the Union, not joining it.** In-tray item 2. **Built that way
already**: the path measures seats, people and economy held **by you**, with no reference to the
remnant's consent.

**W34 — Against W33: the story says the remnant exists and recognises nobody.** *A reunification that
ignores the one government still calling itself the United States is a strange kind of reunification.*

**W35 — Washington's victory should be different because Washington's position is.** §3c: *a big state
starts closer to reunifying than a small one.* **So the path is the same and the distance is not** —
which may be enough, or may be the thin version of item 2.

**W36 — A fourth path: survive.** Sixty-one nations and three ways to win means fifty-eight ways to
lose. **A small nation that is still there in sixty turns has done something**, and the game says
nothing about it.

**W37 — A fifth: be the one nobody could beat.** Win by being unattacked rather than by being large.

**W38 — Victory should be checkable by the player at any moment, with the gap named.** *Already built
— the paths are data with per-condition targets, so "what am I short of" is answerable.*

**W39 — There should be a way to lose that is not being conquered.** *Nothing in the game currently
ends your game except ceasing to exist.*

**W40 — Winning should be rare and the game should be worth playing without it.** Economy mode is
already a sandbox with no win condition and testers are told so.

### E. Time

**W41 — Longer agreements than five years exist, and the story needs them.** In-tray item 3: the
twenty-year deal is eighty turns. **Round 5's ruling 11 put that deal on the opening board and round
5's default was to use the longest existing term instead.** *This round can overturn that.*

**W42 — Against W41: a five-year cap is a design statement.** *Nothing in this game should be settled
for a generation, because a player who signs an eighty-turn deal has removed a decision from the rest
of the game.*

**W43 — The opening memories are dated across the two years, not stamped "two years ago".** In-tray
item 1. **The memory list is dated and decays per turn**, so back-dating to real moments costs nothing
and makes the opening board a history rather than a snapshot.

**W44 — Against W43: eight turns of decay is a narrow window and the difference would be invisible.**
*Worth measuring before anybody builds it.*

**W45 — The calendar should mean something.** Round 6's ruling 4 puts a date on the front page; the
bicentenary is turn 1; four turns is a year and nothing marks a year passing.

**W46 — A game has a length.** Sixty turns is fifteen years. *Nobody has said how long a game is meant
to be, and every tuning decision quietly assumes an answer.*

### F. The Tuesday test, for the whole game

**W47 — Round 7 owns the only version of the question that matters.** Each round answered *what does a
player do about THIS*. **Nobody has asked what a player does on a Tuesday when all six are running at
once** — and the answer today is: one thing, out of roughly thirty they might want to do.

**W48 — The honest count.** Eleven moves exist; the economy wants ten verbs; diplomacy added four;
politics has nine answers to a movement; conquest has its own. **The budget is one.**

**W49 — Triage IS the game, and the interface is where it lives or dies.** If a player must read six
screens to find their one move, the budget is not the problem.

**W50 — The one-action rule has never been played with all six components running**, because they do
not all exist. *Which is an argument for deciding the replacement AFTER the alpha rather than before
it.*

### Ruling 2 — Playing the remnant is a different game. Same victory, opposite stories

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"Playing as Washington should be a different game."* — answering in-tray
item 2, and it is **the first time this design treats one nation's victory differently from
another's.**

**For the federal remnant, reunification means RESTORING what it already claims to be.** For everyone
else it means **REPLACING it** — building the country around yourself until the thing in Washington
still calling itself the United States is irrelevant.

**Rejected, and the near miss is worth recording.** *The remnant's signature as the last condition* —
you hold the seats, the people and the economy and have still not reunified until Washington
acknowledges you. **That would have made the weakest nation on the board the gatekeeper of the biggest
prize, which is exactly the shape round 2 gave Austin in Texas.** *Aaron took the version where the
difference is whose story it is rather than whose permission it needs.*

### What it rests on, verified rather than assumed

**D.C. is a nation on the board: 702,250 people and $184B of output** — by population the smallest
thing on the map that is not a city-state. *So "the remnant" is a real, playable, very small nation
and the asymmetry ruling 2 asks for has somewhere to live.*

**And the machinery is friendlier to this than expected.** *Seats of government* counts **the state
capitals**, and it counts them as `held`, `own` and **`aligned`** — **seats held by nations that share
your politics count toward you.** The engine's own note: *"a beloved hegemon reunifies through nations
it never invaded."* **So reunification is already partly a story about persuasion**, which is exactly
the register the remnant's version needs.

### ⚠ Two things in the story that are NOT built, and ruling 2 depends on both

**Programmer rule 17, applied deliberately this time rather than learned again.**

1. **The remnant as the story describes it does not exist.** Secession §8 has it as Washington **plus
   its martial-law ring, plus the rest of Virginia, plus the Baltimore region.** On the board there is
   **D.C. alone**; Virginia and Maryland are ordinary separate states. *The remnant is one small
   nation, not a rump federal state.*
2. **"Recognises nobody" is not built.** Recognition returns true unconditionally for every nation the
   game opened with, and D.C. is one of them — **so today the remnant recognises everybody.** *This is
   the same class of gap as conquest ruling 19's unbuilt Texas, and it is **round 5's in-tray item
   4**, which the review recovered from having been dropped and which ruling 8 deferred past the
   alpha. **Ruling 2 now depends on it**, so the two travel together.*

**Neither is this round's to build**, and neither changes the ruling. *What changes is that the
ruling is a design for a board the game does not yet have — said plainly, in the document, rather
than discovered by somebody later.*

### Ruling 3 — What you may know depends on the relationship. Knowing things becomes a reason to make friends

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"What you see depends on the relationship."* — answering in-tray items
**5** and **11**, round 5's **T26**, and **C130 with politics ruling 17**, which Aaron deferred on
11 September wanting *"one answer between them."* **This is that answer.**

**An ally's panel is open. A hostile nation's is bands and guesswork.** What a nation may read about
another is gated on how they stand with each other — **and round 5 built the vocabulary this reads**
without knowing it would be used for this.

**⚠ And it gives alliances something nobody had priced.** Round 5's ruling 20 made an alliance a
standing agreement with no term that can only be broken; conquest ruling 27 made it thin on purpose —
inherited quarrels, a lever for lending soldiers, and permission to join a war. **Ruling 3 adds the
first benefit of an alliance that is not military: you can see.** *That is a reason to make friends
with a nation you will never fight beside, which the design did not previously have.*

**Rejected, and two of the three are worth keeping in the bank.** *Keeping one piece of fog and no
more* — cheap, consistent, and it leaves three rounds' questions unanswered. **Bands instead of
figures** (W31) and **information going stale** (W30) — *both remain available as the FORM ruling 3
takes, and neither is excluded by it. The ruling says what gates the view; it does not say what a
restricted view looks like.*

### Four defaults taken, because ruling 3 needs them to mean anything

*Each is reversible and none is a product judgement.*

1. **The ladder is the relationship score that already exists** — Close, Warm, Indifferent, Cold,
   Hostile — **not a new scale.** *The game already computes it for every pair, it already explains
   itself, and a second scale that could disagree with the first is the thing the memory list was
   built to prevent.*
2. **What is physically visible stays visible to everyone.** Ground, borders, population, who is next
   to whom. **You can always see that a nation is large.** *A fog that hides the map is a different
   genre, and W22's objection is right about that much.*
3. **The AI plays under the same restriction.** By the precedent of W12 — the AI gets the same budget
   as the player, whatever it is. *⚠ And this is the expensive half of ruling 3, said plainly: an AI
   that must act on a restricted view is a harder AI to write and a worse one to watch. If it is
   given sight the player does not have, the player is playing against a cheat. **Flagged for the
   technical design director as the real cost of this ruling.***
4. **The unrest map's existing fog is NOT relationship-gated. It stays universal.** *Its reason was
   never trust — it was that the screen must not become an omniscient targeting overlay for the annex
   button. **An ally who turns on you is exactly the danger that reason describes**, so opening it to
   friends would defeat it. Six bands for your own ground and three for everybody else's, whoever
   they are.*

### ⚠ The test this round would rather hand forward than the ruling itself

**The reason written into the one piece of fog that already exists is better than any principle about
what a government would plausibly know:** *not realism — whether a screen becomes a targeting
computer.* **That is answerable about a specific screen by looking at it**, which "what would they
know?" never is. **Finding C, and it is what stage 2 should apply ruling 3 with.**

### Ruling 4 — Time is not the designer's either. Durations and game length go forward with the evidence

**RULED 14 September 2026.** *"This is another thing that the technical designer and I will figure
out."* — answering in-tray item **3** and **finding D** the same way ruling 1 answered A1.

**So this round does not set the duration cap and does not say how long a game is.** *The four options
put to him — a five-year cap with a sixty-turn game, generational agreements with longer games, the
cap alone, and long terms that cost something to sign — are recorded in the bank and none is taken.*

### What round 7 owes on time — the evidence, not the answer

1. **As built:** four turns to a year, opening **1 March 2036**, durations of **2 / 4 / 8 / 20 turns**
   — six months to five years — ruled in **D163**. Memory decays per turn; a new nation's honeymoon is
   four turns.
2. **The story wants eighty turns.** The New England–Rochester free-trade deal is **twenty years**,
   four times the longest term the game allows — **and round 5's ruling 11 put that deal on the
   opening board.** *Round 5 took the reversible default and used the longest existing term instead;
   that default is now explicitly this decision's to confirm or overturn.*
3. **⚠ It is moot on today's board and will not stay moot.** Neither New England nor
   Rochester-and-Buffalo exists in the game — round 5's finding M. *So nothing is blocked today, and
   everything is blocked the moment the story's board is built.*
4. **The design argument on each side, stated once so it need not be re-derived.** *For the cap:
   nothing should be settled for a generation, because a player who signs an eighty-turn deal has
   removed a decision from the rest of the game (**W42**). Against it: the story thinks in decades and
   a five-year ceiling makes every commitment provisional (**W41**).*
5. **Game length is unwritten and every tuning decision assumes an answer.** **Finding D.** Sixty turns
   is fifteen years. *Nobody has finished a game, so there is no measurement to offer — which is
   itself the useful thing to say.*
6. **One question inside this one is a measurement, not a judgement.** In-tray item 1: should the
   opening memories be **dated across the two years** or all stamped *"two years ago"*? **W43** says
   date them — the list is already dated and decays per turn, so it costs nothing and makes the
   opening board a history rather than a snapshot. **W44** says two years is only **eight turns of
   decay**, so the difference may be invisible. *⚠ Measure it before building it: the question is
   whether eight turns of decay separates the oldest grudge from the newest by enough for a player to
   notice.*

### What ruling 2 leaves open, and it is a design question rather than a number

**Is it the same three conditions told two ways, or genuinely different conditions?** *The cheap
version is one path with two narrations — the remnant is simply much closer to the seats and much
further from the people and the economy, so the same targets produce a different game by arithmetic
alone. The expensive version gives the remnant its own condition set.* **Recorded for stage 2, where
the design document is written.**
