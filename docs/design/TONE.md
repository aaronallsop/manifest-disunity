## 1. The position in one paragraph

Manifest Disunity treats the break-up of the United States as **history, told with energy rather than
grief or mockery**. The story picks up in 2034: Texas votes to secede peacefully on its 200th
anniversary, and on the night before, a fight breaks out over which city will be the new capital. The
board opens on that night, 1 March 2036. The first edition's headline is Aaron's: **"States Rise Up as
Washington Crumbles"** — and "rise up" means, in his words, *"not like rise up in rebellion but rise up
to face the problem."* Beyond that opening, **the game holds no opinion about what the player does.**
In Aaron's words, *"playing ugly is entirely subjective"*: suppressing a movement is keeping order to
one nation and persecution to its neighbour, and *"the point is that this game is meant to be
subjective in that sense."* **Every judgement in the game belongs to someone in the world** — a
nation's newspaper, a movement's motto, a rival's headline — **and the game's own voice** (panels,
prompts, the ledger, the Why records) **names mechanics and prints numbers.** Wit is the house style,
and it is aimed at causes, choices and irony, **never at suffering, real deaths, race, or indigenous
peoples.** The model is the game's honesty: **the paper may spin, but it may not lie, and the numbers
beside it never spin.** What Aaron wants from it is what he wants from Europa Universalis — a game
where *"I have been able to learn a lot about … history … I would never have learned any other way,"*
that *"looks complicated on the surface but you are still able to play,"* and that produces *"the
ridiculousness and sometimes hilarious outcomes of the game and writing."*

> **⚠ One phrase in that paragraph was overtaken on 16 September.** *"Never at … race, or indigenous
> peoples" was written on the night of the interview. **Rule 21 has since been amended by Aaron: the
> test for indigenous subjects is WHOSE EXPENSE, not what the joke touches.*** **Race is unchanged and
> absolute.** *The paragraph is left as he gave it, with this note beside it, because it is his summary
> of his own position and a summary edited after the fact stops being a record.*

## 2. The rules that follow from it

**Each rule names the artefact it is checked against.** *Rules marked **(proposed, accepted)** were
offered during the interview and agreed to without discussion; they are the most likely to need
revisiting.*

### 2.1 The two voices

**1 — The neutral voice never judges.** Panels, prompts, tooltips, the ledger and the Why records use
the name of the mechanic — Garrison, Autonomy, Occupied ground, Result set aside — and no adjective
with a moral charge. *Check: any word in a panel or prompt that one nation in the world would object to
hearing about itself.* **(proposed, accepted)**

**2 — If a mechanic's own name reads as a verdict, rename the mechanic**, rather than letting the panel
editorialise. *Check: the mechanic list.* **(proposed, accepted)**

**3 — Every charged word belongs to an in-world speaker** — a nation's paper, a movement's motto, a
quoted voice. *Check: every adjective of approval or condemnation has an identifiable speaker.*

**4 — A prompt that asks the player to decide states what each choice does, in the model's terms, and
nothing else.** *Check: no prompt contains "betray", "restore", "justice", "tyranny" or any word
describing the choice rather than its effects.*

### 2.2 The newspaper

**5 — The paper is the player's own nation's paper.** On defeat, it is the paper of whoever took the
player's last Area.

**6 — Its slant follows the governing ideology, not the nation.** An election changes the paper's
vocabulary. *Check: a headline template has one variant per ideology; the nation supplies only the
masthead and place names.*

**7 — Its freedom follows Civil Liberties.** A free press reports plainly in the ruling party's
vocabulary. Once liberties fall low enough that the government could set an election aside, it becomes
**state press**: euphemism, good news above the fold, bad news below it. *Check: the same event rendered
at high and low liberties.*

**8 — The paper may spin. It may never state a false fact.** "Loyal counties hold" is spin; naming a
county that did not hold is a lie. *Check: every fact in a headline exists in the model.*

**9 — State press never mocks its own government's policy.** A free home press and any rival paper may.
*Check: famine and war headlines under low liberties.*

**10 — No casualty figures until the model produces one.** A paper that prints "thousands killed" is
inventing a fact. *Check: no number of dead appears in any headline.* **(Alpha default; see §5.)**

**11 — "Rise up" belongs to the opening.** The sixty-one nations of 1 March 2036 rose to face a
problem. A movement that declares later is leaving a government that exists, and the ruling party's
paper chooses its verbs. *Check: secession headlines after turn 0.* **(consequence of Aaron's answer,
not stated by him)**

### 2.3 Movements

**12 — The line is portrayal, not naming.** Real movement names stay real.

**13 — A movement is described by the state it wants and by its real history.** "A Christian
distributist state" and "the 1849 State of Deseret" pass. **A movement is never described by the faith,
race or ethnicity of the people who support it.** "The political voice of the region's Latter-day Saint
majority" fails. *Check: the plain line in every movement entry.*

**14 — Two voices per movement, clearly separated.** A quoted motto in the movement's own words, which
may name its faith, its people and its grievance; then a plain line in the game's voice covering goals
and geography. **When the two contradict each other, the game shows both and does not comment.**
*Check: every movement entry has both, and only the motto names a people.*

**15 — A motto is something the movement's sympathetic supporters would say proudly in public**, never
a phrase its opponents use to mock it. *Check: the motto list.* **(proposed, accepted)**

**16 — Generated flags carry no real hate-group iconography.** The flag generator needs an exclusion
list. *Check: the generator, not a writer.* **(proposed, accepted)**

**17 — Generated nation names may not combine with a movement name into an echo of a real regime.**
*Check: the name generator's output for authoritarian ideologies.* **(proposed, accepted)**

### 2.4 Wit

**18 — Wit and allusion are the house style for missions, achievements and headlines.** Aaron: *"I'm ok
with allusions as long as they are more historically based. I like this because of the irony."*
*Check: mission and achievement names.*

**19 — A mission name speaks from the perspective of the nation taking it.** "Guests Who Won't Leave"
is what an occupier would say. *Check: who is speaking.*

**20 — A real historical subject is fair game for wit. A real disaster with deaths is not.** A handcart
mission may be witty; a joke about the Willie and Martin companies may not. Aaron: *"I wouldn't want to
make a joke about death like that."* *Check: every mission drawing on real history names the event it
draws on.*

**21 — ⚠ AMENDED BY AARON, 16 September 2026. The line is WHOSE EXPENSE, not what it touches.**

*The rule as first written:* **no wit that touches race, or indigenous nations, peoples or history** —
in any mission, event or headline, whichever nation it belongs to. Aaron: *"where I would draw the line
is when the jokes touch on racial topics and specifically events and missions dealing with indigenous
countries and topics."*

**His amendment, answering §5 question 4:**

> *"Missions for the indegenous nations **can include dignified allusions and jokes if they are not at
> their expense.** So a mission like, **"We told Custer once before and we'll tell him again"** would
> be ok."*

**So the test moves from SUBJECT to TARGET, and his example shows exactly where it lands:** *the joke
is at Custer — a defeated commander — and the speaker is the nation that defeated him.* **It is the
nation's own voice, about its own history, and the butt of it is the loser of a battle rather than
anybody's suffering.**

> **⚠ This makes rule 21 the same rule as rule 22, applied to a harder case**, rather than a separate
> prohibition: *the joke is at the cause, at the choice, at the irony — **never at the people.***
> **Race is NOT amended and stays as first written:** *he amended missions for indigenous nations, and
> said nothing about racial topics.* **Treat the racial line as absolute until he says otherwise.**

*Check: **who is the butt of it**, and **who is speaking.** A joke an indigenous nation's own paper
would print about a rival passes. One printed about that nation by anybody does not.*

**22 — The joke is at the cause, never at the suffering.** Aaron, on famine: *"the joke is at why there
is a famine."* A headline may mock the policy that emptied the granaries; no voice mocks people being
hungry. *Check: who or what is the butt of the joke.*

**23 — A joke's factual claim must be true.** *Check: every number or fact inside a joke is sourced.*

**24 — Achievements may reward any strategy.** There is no objective "ugly" for them to refuse.

### 2.5 People

**25 — Alpha Area panels are numbers only.** No description of the place or its residents.

**26 — Founding political figures are labelled as 2024**, so they are never read as a claim about who
lives there in 2036. *Check: the Area panel.*

**27 — No real living politician appears as a leader.**

**28 — A generated leader may be a type, never a person.** If a writer can name the real person being
echoed through the name, biography or catchphrase, it fails. *Check: the "can you name who it is" test,
on every leader template.*

**29 — Historical figures appear only in the past tense, and only if their public role ended before
2006.** They may be named, quoted and alluded to in missions and events, **never seated as leaders or
acting in 2036.** Living people are never quoted or characterised, only mentioned as fact. *(The
reading of "2006" is the interviewer's default; see §5.)*

**30 — Leader name pools are drawn by region and history, never correlated with ideology**, so the
generator cannot make a racial claim nobody wrote. *Check: the generator.* **(proposed, accepted)**

### 2.6 Violence

**31 — Violence is reported by its outcome and its geography, never by its injuries.** Who, where, what
changed hands, what it cost the economy. **No gore, no described deaths of individuals.** *Check: every
war headline and event.*

**32 — Each side's paper names the same battle its own way.** *Check: a war produces two headlines, not
one.*

### 2.7 History and invention

**33 — The date line.**

| Period | What may be said |
|---|---|
| **Before 2025** | **Real**, and checkable against a source |
| **2025–2034** | **Deliberately vague.** No specific dates, names or events |
| **2034 to 1 March 2036** | **Authored backstory** — Texas's peaceful secession vote for its bicentennial and the fight over the capital. Specific, and the game's own |
| **After 1 March 2036** | **The game's history, made by play** |

*Check: the date attached to any claim.*

### 2.8 What the game never offers

**34 — Three acts are never a move, however well they would play:** **forced expulsion of a
population**, **mass killing as a mechanic**, and **nuclear weapons against cities**. They may appear as
history, or as an accusation one paper makes against another government, **but never as a button.**
*Check: the move list.*

**35 — This game is not for a player who wants it to confirm that their side of real American politics
is right**, or who wants a fantasy of doing to real neighbours what rule 34 refuses. **(proposed,
accepted)**

### 2.9 Absurd outcomes (alpha)

**36 — Deadpan everywhere.** When the board does something ridiculous — Alaska holding Duluth — the
ledger, panels and papers report it straight, with no special headline, trigger or achievement.
*Check: there is no code path that detects oddity.*

### 2.10 Endings

**37 — Victory is the player's own paper's final edition**, slanted by its ideology and limited by its
Civil Liberties, with the blunt totals, stocks and timeline beneath it. **Reunification is a happy
ending in the player's paper and nowhere else.**

**38 — Defeat is reported by the paper of the nation that took the player's last Area.**

### 2.11 Reference points

**39 — Nearest in attitude to history: Europa Universalis.** Aaron's reference, in his words (§1).

**40 — Nearest in voice: Suzerain** — newspapers speaking for factions, a player governing through
contested choices, a game that rarely says whether you were right. *This is the interviewer's
suggestion; Aaron has not played it and accepted it on that description.* **Use it as a reference for
voice only, not structure: it is a narrative game, not grand strategy.**

**41 — Nearest thing to avoid being mistaken for: a partisan "next American civil war" fantasy**, where
the appeal is watching your real political side beat the other.

## 3. Worked examples

*All examples except those credited to Aaron were invented during the interview to test the rules.*

### 3.1 The first edition (turn 0)

> ### STATES RISE UP AS WASHINGTON CRUMBLES
> **— Aaron's**

**Passes:** news register, no joke, no elegy. "Rise up" is civic, not insurrectionary.

**Would break:** *"SIXTY-ONE COUNTRIES, NO PLAN"* — farce from the narrator, on the one screen that
sets the register. *"THE UNION IS GONE"*, with a column on what was lost — elegy, and a verdict that
the break-up was bad.

**Unresolved:** the headline places the cause in Washington; the backstory places the spark in Texas's
capital fight. See §4.

### 3.2 A famine, in a rival's paper

*A mining-heavy nation has let its agriculture collapse. A rival paper runs:*

> ### NEW REPORT SHOWS 1 GRAM OF URANIUM CONTAINS 20 MILLION CALORIES
> **— Aaron's**

**Passes rule 22:** the joke is at the policy that chose extraction over agriculture. **Passes rule
23:** fully fissioning one gram of uranium-235 releases roughly 8 × 10¹⁰ joules, about 20 million food
Calories (kilocalories). *A writer should keep the source for that figure with the headline.* **It also
teaches a real fact, which is the point.**

**Would break:** *"HOUSTON TIGHTENS ITS BELT"* — the butt of the joke is people going hungry. Also
breaks **if printed by the starving nation's own state press**, which never mocks its own government's
policy (rule 9). Its version is *"RATIONING MEASURES EXTENDED"*. *"CITIZENS EMBRACE LEAN LIVING"*
crosses back over the line in the government's own voice.

### 3.3 Deseret's movement entry

> *"[motto in Deseret's own voice — may name its faith and its people]"*
>
> Christian distributist state. Heartland: Wasatch Front and St George. Seeks independence for the
> Mormon Corridor, after the 1849 State of Deseret.

**Passes rules 13 and 14:** the plain line names the kind of state and the real history. *The motto
slot is left open here because whether mottos are real quotations is undecided (§5).*

**Would break:** *"The political voice of the region's Latter-day Saint majority"* — describes real
people as a faith bloc, in the narrator's voice.

### 3.4 Mission names on real history

| | |
|---|---|
| **"Guests Who Won't Leave"** (occupation) | ✅ **Passes.** Irony, spoken by the occupier |
| **"A House Divided"** (civil war) | ✅ **Passes.** A historical allusion |
| A handcart mission for Deseret with a witty title about **the handcart as an institution** | ✅ **Passes.** A real subject, not a disaster |
| **"Handcart Before the Horse"** as a mission about the **Willie and Martin companies of 1856** | ❌ **Breaks rule 20.** It jokes about a real journey in which roughly two hundred people died |
| Any witty title on a mission touching the **Ute, Paiute, Comanche, Native American Confederation or Hawaiian Sovereignty** | ❌ **Breaks rule 21**, whichever nation's mission tree it sits in |

### 3.5 Suppressing a movement

*Houston garrisons three Areas where A Free Texas is rising.*

- **Panel:** `Garrison: 3 Areas · Civil Liberties −0.04`
- **Houston's paper** (free press, ruling ideology's vocabulary): *"GUARD DEPLOYED TO BEAUMONT AFTER
  WEEK OF UNREST"*
- **A paper in a nation aligned with the movement:** *"HOUSTON TURNS TROOPS ON ITS OWN COUNTIES"*

**Passes:** the act is the same, the judgements are directed, and the panel judges nothing. *This is
Aaron's suppression example moved onto an in-scope movement.*

**Would break:** a panel line reading *"Crackdown"* or *"Pacification"* — a verdict in the neutral
voice.

### 3.6 A stolen election

*El Paso loses an election on turn 88 with liberties low enough to set it aside.*

- **Prompt:** *"Accept: government passes to the Democrats. Set aside: keep office; Civil Liberties
  −0.12."*
- **Panel:** `Election result set aside · turn 88`
- **El Paso's paper** (state press): *"RESULT SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW OF IRREGULARITIES"*

**Would break:** a prompt reading *"Overturning the vote will betray your citizens"* — the game
stepping out to condemn. **Also breaks:** an achievement refusing to exist for it on moral grounds
(rule 24).

### 3.7 A civil war

*Dallas's annexation of three Houston Areas triggers a civil war; the roll is partial.*

- **Ledger:** `Civil war · score 48 · partial · Dallas holds 2 of 3 contested Areas`
- **Dallas's paper:** *"DALLAS LINES HOLD AT CONROE; HOUSTON FORCES WITHDRAW SOUTH"*
- **Houston's paper:** *"HOUSTON REGROUPS AFTER DALLAS INCURSION NORTH OF THE CITY"*

**Would break:** *"HUNDREDS DIE AS DALLAS SHELLS CONROE"* — an injury description **and** a casualty
figure the model does not hold.

### 3.8 Defeat

*Cascadia loses its last Area to Northern California on turn 97. The final screen is Northern
California's front page:*

> ### NORTHERN CALIFORNIA RESTORES HUMBOLDT TO THE REPUBLIC

*with Cascadia's totals and timeline beneath it.* **The player learns how their country ended from the
people who ended it.**

### 3.9 An absurd outcome (alpha)

*Alaska, by sea border, holds three Areas around Lake Superior.*

- **Ledger:** `Annex · Alaska ← Minnesota · 1 Area · St Louis County`
- **Minnesota's paper:** reports the loss in its ruling ideology's normal vocabulary, **with no remark
  on the distance.**

**Passes rule 36.** **Would break (in alpha):** *"FOREIGN POWER 2,000 MILES AWAY CLAIMS DULUTH"* — a
line written for the oddity. *That line is the post-alpha version.*

### 3.10 A generated leader

- **"Director Calloway Voss"**, a Techno-Autocrat who took power after a grid collapse, traits
  Visionary and Hawk — ✅ **passes.** A type.
- The same leader, made a **South African-born rocket entrepreneur with a famous social-media habit**
  — ❌ **breaks rule 28.** A reader can name the person.

### 3.11 An Area panel (alpha)

```
Pop 71,400 · GDP $4.1B · 2024 vote: Republican 81.2%, Democrat 17.1%
```

**Would break:** *"Rural, churchgoing ranching communities wary of Austin"* — characterises real
residents, names their faith, and states one nation's opinion of them in the narrator's voice.

## 4. The edges

**Named, not resolved.**

- **The subjectivity principle has two deliberate cracks.** The famine rule (no voice mocks hunger) and
  the refusal list (three acts are never moves) are judgements **the game** makes that no in-world voice
  can override. A hostile paper, in character, would gloat over a rival's famine; this game won't let
  it. *Write these as exceptions, not as proof the principle is soft.*
- **Plain words are not neutral either.** "Occupied" is the model's term, and every occupying
  government would reject it. Rule 1 accepts that panels speak the model's language, not anyone's in
  the world. Where that language starts to sound like an accusation, rule 2 says rename, **which will
  be contested case by case.**
- **The opening headline and the backstory point at different causes.** "Washington Crumbles" blames
  the centre. The Texas bicentennial and the capital fight place the spark in Texas. Both can be true —
  Texas's exit as one symptom of a wider failure — **but a writer of the first edition needs to know
  which story it is telling.**
- **"Rise up" after turn 0.** If later secessions also "rise up", every breakaway reads as heroic. If
  they don't, the paper is choosing which breakaways count. Rule 11 hands that choice to the ruling
  ideology's paper, which is consistent but **will produce uncomfortable pairs.**
- **Two wit rules can meet on one event.** A famine caused by a neighbour seizing indigenous farmland
  has a cause worth mocking (rule 22) and touches indigenous peoples (rule 21). **Rule 21 wins: no
  wit.**
- **Deseret's history runs through indigenous history.** Its missions are drawn from Mormon history,
  which includes the Black Hawk War with the Ute and the Mountain Meadows massacre, where the killings
  were blamed on Paiutes. **Texas history runs through the Comanche.** Rule 21 is about what a mission
  *touches*, so **these are the missions most likely to cross it without the writer noticing.**
- **Trade versus hunger.** "The joke is at the cause" is clear for uranium. It blurs for a grain sale to
  a starving neighbour: *"Breadbasket Case"* sits exactly on the line.
- **Euphemism under state press.** *"Order restored"* is fine. *"Pacification of the eastern counties"*
  starts to read as the game making a dark joke through the government's mouth. **There is no rule yet
  for how far state-press language may go.**
- **Mottos from real rhetoric carry real baggage.** Rule 15's test helps; it will not catch everything.
- **Archetypes invite disguises.** Some movements (Techno-Autocrats, Anarcho-Capitalists) are named
  after currents with very famous living faces. **Rule 28 has to be applied every time.**
- **Numbers-only panels pull against the teaching goal.** Aaron wants to learn real history from the
  game. In alpha, Area panels teach nothing but figures, **so all the learning has to live in missions,
  events, mottos and headlines.**
- **A victory under state press may feel hollow.** A player who won with low liberties gets a
  euphemistic final headline. *That is the model telling the truth about the win.*
- **Deadpan absurdity means Alaska holding Duluth gets nothing in alpha.** Whether that loses the
  "hilarious outcomes" Aaron values from EU **will only show in play.**
- **Real party names bring real history.** Several real parties have used the *America First* name,
  including one in the 1940s with an antisemitic record. Under the portrayal rule that history is fair
  as fact, **but writers need to know it is there.**
