> **Decided in:** Military, Politics

*The free, mandatory phase at the top of every turn where a movement inside your own country states what it wants and names the ground it wants, the three answers a government may give — grant it, refuse it, or tell them to wait and pay far more later — and the broken-promise clock that turns a strung-along movement into a separatist one.*

## What it is

A movement demand is the moment an organised movement inside a nation stops being background pressure and tells the government what it wants done. Demands are handled before the ordinary turn action and force the government to grant, refuse or delay an answer.

## How it works in the game

A movement's demand follows from its **verb** and, where relevant, its **adjective**. The politics design ties the six movement verbs to a small set of government responses rather than inventing a unique minigame for every movement ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 11]]).

Demands appear in a mandatory phase before the nation's ordinary action. The player must answer them, but choosing the answer itself is free ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 12]]). Carrying out the promise can still cost whatever the underlying act normally costs; that default was later confirmed ([[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 41]]).

The military design gives every demand **three answers**: grant it, refuse it, or tell the movement to wait ([[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 16]]). Refusal makes the movement grow faster; repeatedly stringing it along is worse, because enough broken promises can make the movement change what it wants entirely ([[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 30]]; [[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 22]]).

Once a movement changes its objective because of broken promises, it does not change back ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 22]]). The number of promises that must be broken before this happens is deliberately unset.

A **Unify** movement provides the clearest example. Its demand is not that the map merge automatically; it tells its government to approach another nation and propose a [[Unions|union]] ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 15]]). Making the proposal discharges the immediate demand even if the other nation refuses, after which the movement can return later pointing toward a different target ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 18]]).

Under the current one-action turn structure, actually making that union proposal consumes the nation's action ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 19]]). That cost is conditional on the one-action rule and must be revisited when the turn system is redesigned.

Movements demanding external action can name concrete ground or infrastructure. The military design includes fixed [[Military bases|military bases]] as geography, so an expansionist demand can care about a place such as NORAD or Pearl Harbor rather than only about an abstract amount of territory ([[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 31]]).

A nation's diplomatic environment can also feed movement pressure. Hostile-neighbour matching movements grow faster under [[Hostility]], but that effect switches off completely once the relationship cools to Wary ([[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 9]]; [[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 40]]).

When combined movement share passes the separate national threshold, the country starts the [[Civil war]] countdown; this is distinct from the Area-level threshold at which one piece of ground can leave ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 8]]).

The entire demand screen, promise clock and verb-changing machinery remain unbuilt.

## The story behind it

The Farmers Union supplies the printable moment the system is aiming for: its supporters are no longer merely "growing"; they tell the government to go to another state and ask for union. Elsewhere, the Front Range movement can care about NORAD, Hawaiian Sovereignty can point at scarce resources and Pearl Harbor, and Gulf movements can point at Louisiana ground held by Houston. The point is to turn political pressure into concrete choices on the map.

## Questions for the designer

- **Demand trigger:** the percentage at which a movement begins issuing demands is deliberately unset. It must be below 0.30 if movements capped at 0.30 are ever to use the system.
- **Broken-promise count:** ruling 22 deliberately leaves the number unset.
- **One-action dependency:** proposing a union currently costs the ordinary action only because the game currently gives one action; revisit when that turn rule changes.
- **Verb-change implementation:** no existing system changes a movement's verb. This is explicitly new machinery.
- **Generated source metadata:** several live military rulings on this page are wrongly labelled superseded by the generator.
