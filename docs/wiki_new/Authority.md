*How firmly a government holds its own ground — what raises it, what drags it down, the four turns of borrowed patience a brand-new country is lent, and what a nation spends when it kneels to somebody else.*

## What it is

Authority is how firmly a government holds its own country together. It is one of the stocks that makes a government look strong or weak to its own people and to other nations, and it can be spent, damaged or temporarily propped up by political conditions.

## How it works in the game

Authority is part of the game's wider measure of whether a government is "doing well". [[Grievance]] reads it alongside Quality of Life and Influence, so a government that cannot hold itself together creates political pressure even when other conditions are acceptable ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 13]]).

Authority also matters outside the country. When another nation considers a political proposal such as a [[Unions|union]], the answer is shaped by the other country's appetite, the proposer's weight and the relationship between them; Authority and Influence are among the stocks already available to that decision ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 17]]).

A new country receives a temporary political honeymoon. The running game already contains **four turns of borrowed Authority**, while the later design interprets that honeymoon as public tolerance for the new state's early failures. That tolerance is supposed to fade and eventually leave the country worse than neutral for a period, but the depth and duration of that after-effect were deliberately left unset ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 38]]).

The federal remnant is not meant to receive an arbitrary opening Authority penalty. Its weakness should emerge from the real losses and political damage written into the opening world — recent losses, age and tenure all already feed the relevant systems ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 24]]).

Joining a [[Federation]] is a deliberate trade between domestic control and external reach. Membership costs Authority while increasing Influence, so the country becomes less sovereign internally in exchange for more weight abroad ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 37]]). The federation's fixed internal toll is likewise a standing cost of choosing membership rather than an ordinary negotiated corridor ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 26]]).

[[Release valves|Martial law]] is another direct political intervention. It relaxes ordinary constraints rather than simply adding soldiers, but whether declaring it consumes the nation's one action is explicitly deferred to the later turn-structure redesign ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 14]]).

A future vassal or subject system is meant to leave the subordinate government in place. Its people therefore continue blaming that local government for conditions rather than transferring all grievance to the overlord ([[Rulings - Politics#Politics ruling 39]]). The subject relationship itself remains deferred and unbuilt.

## The story behind it

Oklahoma submitting to Dallas is the clearest story example of Authority being spent in exchange for survival. The new-country honeymoon supplies the opposite picture: citizens accept failures for a few turns because the state is new and politically charged, then that tolerance disappears. The federal remnant begins at the other end of the spectrum, carrying the accumulated political damage of recent territorial and institutional losses.

## Questions for the designer

- **New-country after-effect:** ruling 38 says the honeymoon ends worse than neutral, but how far below baseline and for how long are deliberately unset.
- **Visibility:** ruling 17 uses Authority and Influence in another nation's decision, but whether the player may see those stocks before making the proposal was deferred.
- **Martial-law action cost:** deliberately deferred until the one-action turn structure is redesigned.
- **Subject/vassal details:** ruling 39 only establishes who is blamed; the actual subject relationship is owned by the later diplomacy round.
