*The three kinds of installation that sit on the map as geography — Army, Air Force and Naval — what each gives whoever holds it, why the naval one pays a nation that never fights, and why there are no nuclear weapons.*

## What it is

Military bases are fixed installations on the map. They are geography a nation can control rather than movable units, and they give different strategic benefits depending on whether the installation is Army, Air Force or Naval.

## How it works in the game

The design recognises three base types: **Army, Air Force and Naval** ([[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 31]]). Bases stay attached to their location on [[The board]] and therefore change hands when control of that ground changes.

Army and Air Force installations contribute to the military value of the nation holding them. Naval installations also matter economically and strategically even for a nation that is not actively fighting, because access to ports and naval infrastructure affects how that country connects to the wider map ([[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 31]]).

The same ruling makes a separate, final scope decision: **Manifest Disunity has no nuclear weapons**. That is a deliberate design choice, not an omitted feature waiting for a later military round ([[Rulings - Military#Military ruling 31]]).

No military-base dataset or mechanics are currently built. The source names installations such as Pearl Harbor, NORAD/Cheyenne Mountain, Pensacola, Norfolk, Bremerton and Groton as authored material for the eventual map pass.

## The story behind it

The Shattering leaves military infrastructure inside countries that did not build it and may not share the old federal strategy. Pearl Harbor makes Hawaii strategically valuable; NORAD places an important installation inside the Front Range story; Pensacola helps explain why the Gulf coast matters. The absence of nuclear weapons is intentional so that control of these installations creates leverage without turning the game into nuclear escalation.
